\section{Introduction} 
With more than 21 billion connected Internet of Things (IoT) devices projected worldwide by the end of 2025~\cite{iotanalytics2025devices}, large-scale IoT deployments continue to expand across healthcare, smart homes, and industrial automation. However, intrusion detection in these heterogeneous and resource-constrained networks remains challenging. While machine learning (ML)-based IDS can model complex traffic patterns, most existing approaches assume a closed-set setting and overfit known attack categories. As a result, detection rates for unseen or zero-day attacks can drop by up to 70\%~\cite{Sarhan2023ZeroShot}. Meanwhile, generative AI technologies are rapidly accelerating the creation of novel exploits and malware variants, further increasing the prevalence of previously unknown attacks~\cite{Ferrag2025GenAICybersec,Xu2025LLMAutonomousAttack}. Rather than extending closed-set classifiers that bias decision boundaries toward known attack distributions, we argue that \emph{one-class novelty detection} (OCND) provides a more suitable formulation for IoT intrusion detection under incomplete attack knowledge: it models only normal traffic and flags any significant deviation as a potential intrusion

\subsection{Challenges}
Deploying OCND in real-world IoT environments introduces three practical challenges: 
\begin{itemize} 
\item \textbf{Imperfect benign supervision.} Benign training data are rarely perfectly clean due to labeling limitations and open-world uncertainty. Even slight contamination from annotation errors can distort learned representations~\cite{Prabowo2026UncleanTrainingNIDS}.

\item \textbf{Diverse anomaly structures.} IoT anomalies arise from heterogeneous, multi-source distributions and do not follow a single structural type. Consequently, detector performance varies substantially across local, global, and clustered anomalies (Fig.~\ref{fig:ADBench_bar_chart})~\cite{han2022adbench,KANG2019772}.
\item \textbf{Edge deployment constraints.} IoT IDS require scalability in training and efficiency in inference, favoring lightweight methods over computationally intensive deep models~\cite{Rahman2025IoTIDSSurvey,beggel2019robust,jodelka2021adaptive}.
\end{itemize} 

These challenges do not occur in isolation; rather, they interact in ways that fundamentally constrain the design of OCND models. \textbf{The first trade-off}, arising from imperfect benign supervision and heterogeneous anomaly structures, concerns the balance between preserving the intrinsic geometry of benign traffic and maintaining tolerance to contamination in the training data. Boundary-based approaches that tightly capture data geometry achieve high sensitivity but become fragile under even mild label noise~\cite{perera2019ocgan,han2022adbench,DeepSVDD}. \textbf{The second trade-off} arises from deployment constraints and concerns scalability versus inference efficiency. Deep representation methods often require substantial computational resources, while lightweight proximity-based approaches scale well but suffer from expensive test-time distance computation~\cite{han2022adbench,Rahman2025IoTIDSSurvey,KNN}. Consequently, existing approaches address only subsets of these challenges and have yet to resolve them jointly within a unified framework.

\subsection{Proposed Approach}
To address these challenges, we propose a unified lightweight OCND framework that jointly tackles both trade-offs.

\textbf{(i) Structure-preserving representation with controlled sensitivity to contamination.}
We introduce a one-class Fisher-inspired null-space projection that suppresses intra-class variation while preserving the intrinsic geometry of normal data. 
By mapping samples into a null space, the method enables a simple global threshold to characterize normal behavior, while the resulting compact representation reduces sensitivity to mild contamination.


\textbf{(ii) Efficient distance-based inference.}
In the projected space, anomaly detection is performed using a simple linear distance-based scoring rule. To reduce the inference cost of proximity-based methods, samples are grouped into pseudo-classes and summarized by compact prototypes, enabling efficient inference while preserving global structural consistency. 


Although null-space projection provides a natural mechanism for compact representation, classical formulations such as the Null Foley–Sammon Transform become numerically unstable or even undefined in the high-sample, low-dimension regime ($n \gg d$), which is common in IoT network traffic data. To overcome this limitation, we develop a near-exact SVD-based construction that ensures numerical robustness and makes null-space modeling practical. Together, these components form a OCND framework that preserves geometric structure, reduces the impact of contamination, and supports efficient edge-level deployment.



\subsection{Main Contributions}
The main contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{itemize} 
\item We formally characterize IoT intrusion detection as a benign-only one-class novelty detection (OCND) problem, providing a novel perspective for intrusion detection in open-set environments where attack categories cannot be exhaustively enumerated. Under this formulation, we identify two fundamental trade-offs: structure preservation versus contamination sensitivity, and training scalability versus inference efficiency.

\item We propose \textbf{XXX}, a lightweight OCND framework that combines one-class null-space projection with prototype-based distance scoring. The method preserves benign structure, diminishes the impact of mild contamination, and supports efficient inference in edge deployments. 

\item We conduct extensive experiments on four standard IoT benchmarks (CICIoT2023, ToN-IoT, NBaIoT, and BoT-IoT), including controlled evaluations under synthetic local, cluster, and global anomaly types as well as benign-set contamination rates of 1\%, 3\%, and 5\%. The results show that \textbf{XXX} delivers strong and consistent performance across all scenarios. 
\end{itemize}

Intrusion Detection Systems (IDSs) are essential for securing IoT networks against evolving threats such as distributed denial-of-service attacks, botnet activity, and probing. Because IoT network flows are typically represented as tabular features, a wide range of Anomaly Detection (AD) algorithms have been widely adopted as standard baselines in IoT security ~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune,samara2022survey}, where AD is formally defined as the task of ``finding data points that do not conform with the rest of the data, and such data points are referred to as anomalies or outliers''~\cite{samariya2023comprehensive}. Beyond anomaly-based detection, two other paradigms are commonly employed: \emph{signature-based} methods match traffic against known-attack rule databases and achieve low false-alarm rates but cannot detect zero-day or polymorphic threats~\cite{liao2013intrusion}; and \emph{hybrid} methods combine both layers to balance precision and coverage, though they still incur the maintenance burden of the signature component~\cite{khraisat2019survey}. Compared with signature-based and hybrid counterparts, AD-driven solutions are inherently better suited to open-world settings where attack patterns are incomplete and heterogeneous~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}.


\subsection{Anomaly Detection for IoT Intrusion Detection}

Along a wide range of general-purpose Anomaly Detection (AD) algorithms, a limited number of specialized frameworks explicitly tailored to the intrinsic characteristics of IoT ecosystems
Existing general-purpose models evaluated in this domain can be categorized into shallow and deep methods \ref{fig:relatedWorks}.

Shallow models, including density-based (e.g., LOF), statistical (e.g., ECOD), and ensemble-based methods (e.g., Isolation Forest), are computationally efficient and easy to deploy~\cite{KNN,9338429,IForest}. However, their limited representational capacity often fails to capture the heterogeneous and multimodal structure of benign IoT traffic, leading to unstable performance.

Deep methods improve expressiveness through reconstruction-based models (e.g., Autoencoders), compactness-driven objectives (e.g., DeepSVDD), and adversarial frameworks (e.g., ALAD)~\cite{Autoencoder,ALAD}. While effective in modeling complex distributions, they introduce significant computational overhead and require careful tuning, limiting their practicality in resource-constrained environments. This reflects a fundamental trade-off between representation power and deployment efficiency.

Moreever, IoT-specific architectures improve deployment feasibility through online ensemble learning (e.g., Kitsune), distributed self-learning profiles (e.g., DIoT, FLiForest), and lightweight gateway-centric designs (e.g., Passban)~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune, nguyen2019diot, xiang2026federated, 8976157}. While these IoT-specific architectures significantly improve deployment feasibility, they often require complex distributed training paradigms or rely on ensembles of neural networks that still incur non-trivial computational overhead during continual model updates.

\subsection{One-Class Novelty Detection Methods}

In open-world IoT security, intrusion detection is often formulated as a one-class novelty detection (OCND) problem, where models are trained on benign data only. Classical methods such as One-Class SVM estimate a decision boundary, while DeepSVDD learns a compact latent representation of normality~\cite{shin2005one,DeepSVDD}.
However, these approaches rely on a global notion of normality, implicitly assuming that benign data forms a single coherent structure. This assumption is often violated in IoT environments, where normal traffic spans multiple operating regimes. As a result, such models may either overfit to dominant patterns or miss locally structured anomalies.

Null-space-based methods provide an alternative perspective. Kernel NFST projects data into a discriminative null space by collapsing intra-class variance and measuring deviations in the projected space~\cite{bodesheim2013kernel}. Extensions such as nNFST further improve structure preservation for intrusion detection~\cite{nguyen2025nnfst}. Nevertheless, these methods are primarily designed for supervised or multiclass settings and do not naturally extend to benign-only learning, nor do they explicitly address contamination or scalability.

\textbf{Summary.} Existing methods address representation, robustness, and efficiency largely in isolation. There remains a lack of a unified framework that simultaneously captures heterogeneous benign structure, maintains robustness under slight contamination, and operates efficiently at scale. This gap motivates the proposed approach.

\section{Problem Formulation}

\subsection{Problem Definition}
\label{sec:problem_def}

Consider an IoT network monitoring environment with feature space $\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}$. Let $\mathbb{P}_{\text{norm}}$ denote the distribution of benign network traffic over $\mathcal{X}$. In the one-class setting, we observe a training set
\[
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}=\{x_1,\ldots,x_N\}, \qquad x_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
\]
and seek to learn a scoring function
\[
S:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
\]
such that benign samples receive low scores, while anomalous samples receive high scores.

Given a decision threshold $\tau$, a test sample $x_{\text{test}}$ is classified as
\begin{equation}
f(x_{\text{test}})=
\begin{cases}
0, & \text{if } S(x_{\text{test}})\le \tau,\\
1, & \text{if } S(x_{\text{test}})> \tau,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:ocnd_decision}
\end{equation}
where label $0$ denotes benign traffic and label $1$ denotes anomalous traffic. In practice, $\tau$ is typically chosen from the training-score distribution according to a fixed contamination assumption. 

While this formulation is standard, practical IoT intrusion detection departs from the ideal OCND setting in three important ways.

\paragraph{1. Slight contamination in the benign training set.}
In real deployments, the training set may not be perfectly clean. Undetected attacks or annotation bias can introduce a small number of anomalous samples into the nominal training data. A convenient way to express this is to model the training samples as being drawn from a contaminated distribution
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{P}_{\text{train}}=(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{P}_{\text{norm}}+\varepsilon \mathbb{P}_{\text{cont}},
\qquad 0\le \varepsilon \ll 1,
\label{eq:contaminated_train}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{P}_{\text{cont}}$ denotes the contamination distribution. 
% The resulting challenge is to reduce sensitivity to this slight contamination.

\paragraph{2. Multimodal benign structure and heterogeneous anomaly manifestations.}
Benign IoT traffic rarely follows a single homogeneous pattern because devices, protocols, and usage contexts can induce multiple operating regimes. A convenient abstraction is to model benign traffic as a mixture distribution
\begin{align}
\mathbb{P}_{\text{norm}}(x)
&= \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, \mathbb{P}_k(x \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k), \\
\pi_k &\ge 0,\quad \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k = 1.
\label{eq:mixture_norm}
\end{align}
where each component $\mathbb{P}_k$ represents a latent benign traffic pattern with mean $\mu_k$ and covariance $\Sigma_k$.

Under such multimodal benign structure, anomalous behavior may appear in different forms, including local anomalies that deviate from the neighborhood structure of a benign mode, clustered anomalies that form coherent abnormal groups, and global anomalies that lie far from the overall benign support. Effective IoT novelty detection therefore requires more than a single global notion of deviation.

\paragraph{3. Deployment-oriented efficiency constraints.}
IoT intrusion detection systems are often deployed in large-scale or edge environments where efficiency is a fundamental requirement. This leads to two main constraints: scalability in training, where the learning process must remain feasible when handling large-scale IoT tabular datasets; and (ii) efficient inference, where the trained model must support continuous prediction. These two aspects are essential for practical deployment alongside detection performance.

\paragraph{Problem statement.}
Given a training set drawn primarily from a multimodal benign distribution but subject to slight contamination, our goal is to learn a lightweight anomaly scoring function and decision rule that:
(i) preserves benign structure sufficiently well to support benign-only novelty detection,
(ii) exhibits limited sensitivity to slight training contamination,
(iii) enables scalable training and fast inference speed in practical deployment settings.
\subsection{Challenges of NFST in OCND}
\label{sec:nfst_challenges}

The Null Foley--Sammon Transform (NFST) is a discriminative linear projection method derived from the Fisher criterion. Given a projection direction $w$, the function objective is
\begin{equation}
F(w)=\frac{w^{\top}S_b w}{w^{\top}S_w w},
\label{eq:fisher}
\end{equation}
where $S_b$ and $S_w$ denote the between-class and within-class scatter matrices, respectively. For a multiclass training set with class means $\mu_j$, global mean $\mu$, and class size $n_j$, these matrices are
\begin{align}
S_b &= \sum_{j=1}^{c} n_j(\mu_j - \mu)(\mu_j - \mu)^{\top}, \\
S_w &= \sum_{j=1}^{c} \sum_{i=1}^{n_j} (x_{ij} - \mu_j)(x_{ij} - \mu_j)^{\top}.
\label{eq:scatter}
\end{align}

NFST seeks projection directions that annihilate the within-class scatter while preserving between-class separation:
\begin{equation}
w^{\top}S_w w = 0,
\qquad
w^{\top}S_b w > 0.
\label{eq:nfst_constraint}
\end{equation}
Directions satisfying Eq.~(\ref{eq:nfst_constraint}) are called \emph{null projecting directions} (NPDs).

Let $X_t=[x_1-\mu,\ldots,x_N-\mu]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}$ be the centered data matrix, and let $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ be an orthonormal basis for $\mathrm{span}(X_t)$. Any projection vector can be written as $w=Q\beta$, which leads to the reduced null-space condition
\begin{equation}
Q^{\top}S_w Q\,\beta = 0.
\label{eq:nullspace}
\end{equation}
Therefore, valid NPDs exist only if the null space $\mathcal{N}(Q^{\top}S_w Q)$ is nontrivial and contains directions that still preserve positive between-class scatter.

Although NFST is effective in multiclass discrimination, two difficulties arise when it is transferred to benign-only novelty detection.

\paragraph{1. Degeneracy under one-class training.}
Classical NFST fundamentally relies on between-class scatter. In benign-only training, however, there is only one observed class ($c=1$). In that case, the global mean coincides with the class mean, so $S_b=0$. Consequently, the discriminative condition $w^{\top}S_b w>0$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:nfst_constraint}) cannot be satisfied, and the original NFST objective degenerates. This means that classical NFST cannot be applied directly for this task.

\paragraph{2. Absence and fragility of null directions in large tabular settings.}
In large tabular regimes (e.g., $N \gg d$), the matrix $Q^{\top}S_w Q$ may become full rank within the total-scatter subspace, yielding no exact null directions. Even when near-null directions exist, their recovery is often numerically unstable due to basis construction and floating-point errors. Consequently, adapting NFST to benign-only IoT novelty detection requires more than a direct reuse of the classical formulation; it calls for surrogate discriminative structure and numerically stable projection mechanisms.

\subsection{Deployment Architecture}
\label{subsec:deployment_workflow} 

In practical deployment, XXX operates on flow-level or session-level IoT traffic features rather than raw packets. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:workflow_system}, packet streams are first captured from heterogeneous IoT devices through edge gateways (e.g., WiFi), then aggregated into flows or sessions and encoded into fixed-dimensional feature vectors. While the end-to-end pipeline reflects a real-world system, the method is based on tabular feature representation consistent with the benchmark datasets used in our experiments. From a system perspective, we summarize the proposed framework into three basic components:


\paragraph{Data preparation}
Raw packet streams are aggregated into flows and transformed via protocol-aware encoding. In particular, categorical protocol attributes are embedded into numerical representations, followed by variance stabilization and feature scaling to ensure all samples lie in a consistent \(d\)-dimensional feature space. This step bridges raw network traffic and learning-ready tabular data.

\paragraph{Offline model training}
The model is trained using only benign traffic. A one-class filtering step is first applied to ensure clean normal data, optionally verified via human labeling or heuristic rules. The filtered benign data is then partitioned into pseudo-classes to support subspace learning. Based on these pseudo-classes, XXX learns a projection matrix \(W\) and corresponding subspace representation for downstream detection. Representative prototypes (e.g., class centroids in the projected space) and a decision threshold are also estimated.

\paragraph{Online detection at the edge}
During deployment, incoming traffic samples are first projected into the learned subspace via $z = W^\top x.$ The projected sample is then compared against the learned benign prototypes to compute a novelty score, reflecting its deviation from normal behavior. This score is evaluated against a pre-defined threshold to determine whether the sample is normal or anomalous. If an anomaly is detected, an alert is triggered at the edge for real-time response.

This separation between cloud-based training and lightweight edge inference enables scalable and efficient deployment in IoT environments.


\subsection{Algorithmic Overview of XXX}
\label{subsec:overview_XXX}

Let \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} = \{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}\) be a set of benign training samples, where each \(x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\). We denote the corresponding data matrix by \(X = [x_1, \ldots, x_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\). Given only benign data, XXX aims to learn a projection matrix \(W\) together with a novelty scoring function \(s(\cdot)\). Figure~\ref{fig:workflow_algorithm} summarizes the proposed pipeline, which is organized into three main stages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Structure-inducing pseudo-class construction.}  
    XXX partitions benign data into \(c\) pseudo-classes via K-means to induce a representative multi-modal structure, enabling downstream discriminative projection rather than optimizing clustering accuracy.

    \item \textbf{Spectral relaxation near-null subspace projection.}  
    To overcome the absence of exact null-space solutions in full-rank IoT data, XXX replaces strict NFST constraints with a SVD-based near-null subspace defined by small within-class eigenvalues, ensuring numerical stability while suppressing intra-class variation.

    \item \textbf{Prototype-aligned novelty scoring.}  
    In the projected space, each pseudo-class is summarized by its centroid, and novelty is computed as the minimum distance to these prototypes, followed by a global threshold estimated from benign score distribution for efficient inference.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Structure-Inducing Pseudo-Class Construction}
\label{subsec:pseudoclass_XXX}

\noindent {\textbf{Insight.}} XXX applies a lightweight pseudo-class construction mechanism that partitions benign samples into coarse groups. The objective is not to recover an exact clustering structure, but rather to obtain a computationally efficient approximation that captures the underlying multi-modal distribution. In addition, this mechanism mitigates the degeneracy issue of NFST under one-class training settings.

Formally, we divide the benign training set into \(c\) pseudo-classes (\(c \ge 2\)), where \(C_j\) denotes the \(j\)-th pseudo-class and \(n_j = |C_j|\):
\[
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} = \bigcup_{j=1}^{c} C_j,
\qquad
C_j \cap C_\ell = \varnothing \quad \text{for } j \neq \ell .
\]
The corresponding centroids and mean are defined:
\[
\mu_j=\frac{1}{n_j}\sum_{x_i\in C_j}x_i,
\qquad
\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i .
\]

\subsection{Spectral Relaxation of the NFST Near-null Space}
\label{subsec:projection_XXX}


\noindent \textbf{Insight.}
Classical NFST relies on the existence of exact null directions of the within-class scatter matrix. However, in practical high-dimensional tabular settings, the within-class scatter matrix is often numerically full-rank, causing the exact null-space assumption to break down. To address this limitation, we propose a spectral relaxation that constructs a near-null subspace from the low-energy eigenspectrum of the within-class scatter operator. This relaxation guarantees the existence of a valid solution even when the exact null space is absent.


Let the centered data matrix be $X_c=[x_1-\mu,\ldots,x_n-\mu]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},$
with compact singular value decomposition
\begin{equation}
X_c = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^\top ,
\label{eq:svd_XXX_revised}
\end{equation}
where \(U_r\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\) spans the total-scatter subspace induced by the data. Any projection direction is therefore restricted to this subspace and can be expressed as \(w = U_r \beta\). Accordingly, the within-class and between-class scatter matrices are reduced to
\begin{equation}
M = U_r^\top S_w U_r, 
\qquad 
A = U_r^\top S_b U_r .
\label{eq:reduced_scatter_XXX}
\end{equation}
Instead of requiring exact null directions, we construct a \emph{near-null subspace} spanned by eigenvectors associated with the smallest eigenvalues of \(M\), where \(0 \le \lambda_1 \le \cdots \le \lambda_r\) denote the eigenvalues of \(M\). 
\[
M = V \Lambda V^\top .
\]
% Classical Null Foley--Sammon Transform (NFST) assumes the existence of a non-trivial null space of \(M\), namely projection directions satisfying \(Mw=0\). However, in practical IoT tabular data, the within-class scatter matrix \(S_w\) is often full-rank due to the small-sample regime (\(n<d\)). Consequently, the exact null space may not exist, which makes the classical NFST formulation inapplicable. In particular, direct null-space extraction or Gram--Schmidt-based constructions can only operate when a true null subspace is already present and therefore cannot remove this rank constraint.

% To overcome this limitation, XXX introduces a \emph{spectral relaxation} of the NFST constraint. The complete procedure is summarized in ư








\begin{definition}[Near-null subspace]
Given a relaxation parameter \(\epsilon \ge 0\), the near-null index set :
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I}_\epsilon =
\left\{\ell : \lambda_\ell \le \epsilon\,\lambda_{\max}(M)\right\}.
\label{eq:near_null_index_XXX}
\end{equation}
The corresponding near-null subspace is spanned by
\[
B_\epsilon = V_{[:,\mathcal{I}_\epsilon]} .
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
Let \(M\) be symmetric positive semidefinite. For any \(\epsilon > 0\), the near-null index set \(\mathcal{I}_\epsilon\) is guaranteed to be non-empty.
\end{remark}
Within this near-null subspace, discriminative structure is further enhanced by maximizing the projected between-class scatter. After applying the near-null index set \(\mathcal{I}_\epsilon\), we have \(A_\epsilon = B_\epsilon^\top A B_\epsilon\), with eigendecomposition \(A_\epsilon = R \Gamma R^\top\). Eigenvectors associated with positive eigenvalues are retained and denoted by \(R_+\). The final projection matrix is therefore
\begin{equation}
W = U_r B_\epsilon R_+ \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times L}.
\label{eq:projection_XXX_revised}
\end{equation}
A sample \(x\) is projected by the matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times L}\):
\[
z = W^\top x .
\]
Overall, the proposed method replacing the infeasible exact null-space constraint with a spectral relaxation that identifies directions of minimal within-class variance Algorithm~\ref{alg:nearnull_nfst}.

% \end{proof}

\subsection{Prototype-aligned novelty scoring}
\label{subsec:scoring_XXX}
\noindent \textbf{Insight.}
Conventional distance-based methods require computing distances between a test sample and all training samples, which is computationally expensive during inference. In contrast, after projection, the proposed formulation localizes each class into a compact representation in the embedded space. As a result, each class can be effectively represented by a single prototype. To reduce inference cost, we compute the score by measuring distances between the test sample and these compact class representations.
\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Spectral Relaxation NFST Solver}
\label{alg:nearnull_nfst}
\begin{algorithmic}
\REQUIRE Data matrix \(X\), scatter matrices \(S_w,S_b\), global mean \(\mu\), tolerance \(\epsilon\)
\ENSURE Projection matrix \(W\)

\STATE Center data matrix \(X_c \gets X-\mu\mathbf{1}^\top\)

\STATE Compute compact SVD: \(X_c = U_r \Sigma_r V_r^\top\)

\STATE Compute:
\(M \gets U_r^\top S_w U_r,\;
A \gets U_r^\top S_b U_r\)

\STATE Eigen-decomposition: \(M = V \Lambda V^\top\)

\STATE Extract near-null index set: $\mathcal{I}_\epsilon $, \(B_\epsilon \gets V_{[:,\mathcal{I}_\epsilon]}\)

\STATE Compute:
\(A_\epsilon \gets B_\epsilon^\top A B_\epsilon\)

\STATE Eigen-decomposition: \(A_\epsilon = R \Gamma R^\top\)

\STATE Retain positive eigenvalues \(R_+\)

\STATE \(W \gets U_r B_\epsilon R_+\)

\RETURN \(W\)

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

After learning \(W\), each pseudo-class is represented in the projected space by its projected centroid \(p_j = W^\top \mu_j, \; j=1,\ldots,c\), and given a test sample \(x_{\text{test}}\), let \(z_{\text{test}} = W^\top x_{\text{test}}\), its novelty score is defined as the minimum Euclidean distance to the projected pseudo-class representatives:
\begin{equation}
s(x_{\text{test}})
=
\min_{j=1,\ldots,c}
\left\| W^\top (x_{\text{test}} - \mu_j) \right\|_2.
\label{eq:novelty_score_XXX_revised}
\end{equation}
To convert novelty scores into binary decisions, XXX first computes training scores for benign samples:
\begin{equation}
s_i
=
\min_{j=1,\ldots,c}
\left\|
W^\top x_i - p_j
\right\|_2,
\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.
\label{eq:train_scores_XXX_revised}
\end{equation}
The decision threshold is then estimated from the empirical distribution of these scores:
\begin{equation}
\tau = Q_{1-\rho}\big(\{s_i\}_{i=1}^{n}\big),
\label{eq:tau_XXX_revised}
\end{equation}
where \(Q_{1-\rho}\) denotes the \((1-\rho)\)-quantile and \(\rho\) controls how much of the upper tail of benign training scores is ignored. The special case \(\rho=0\) reduces to the maximum training score. The final decision rule:

% This score is intentionally prototype-based. It captures multiple benign modes at low computational cost, but it does not explicitly model within-mode covariance or local density. Accordingly, XXX should be interpreted as a lightweight multi-representative detector rather than a full covariance-aware local model.




\begin{equation}
f(x_{\text{test}})=
\begin{cases}
0, & \text{if } s(x_{\text{test}})\le \tau,\\
1, & \text{if } s(x_{\text{test}})> \tau.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:decision_rule_XXX_revised}
\end{equation}
Hence, XXX combines \emph{representative-based localized scoring} with a \emph{single global threshold} (Figure~\ref{fig:threshold_rewrite}). 

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{pictures/noveltyScoring.pdf}
    \caption{Illustration of threshold estimation in XXX. After representative-based scoring is computed for benign training samples, a single global threshold is selected from the empirical score distribution using the quantile parameter \(\rho\).}
    \label{fig:threshold_rewrite}
\end{figure}

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Inference Procedure of \textbf{XXX}}
\label{alg:test_locnfst}
\begin{algorithmic}
\REQUIRE Test sample \(x\in\mathbb{R}^d\), projection matrix \(W\), prototypes \(\{p_j\}_{j=1}^{c}\), threshold \(\tau\)
\ENSURE Predicted label

\STATE Compute projected representation
\[
z \gets W^\top x
\]

\STATE Compute novelty score
\[
s(x)=\min_{j=1,\ldots,c}\|z-p_j\|_2
\]

\IF{$s(x) \le \tau$}
\STATE return \texttt{normal}
\ELSE
\STATE return \texttt{anomalous}
\ENDIF

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Complexity Analysis}
\label{subsec:complexity_XXX_revised}

Let \(n\) denote the number of training samples, \(d\) the feature dimension, \(c\) the number of pseudo-classes, and \(L \le d\) the null-space dimension. The pseudo-class construction stage using K-means requires \(O(ncdI)\), while the projection learning stage is dominated by scatter computation and compact SVD operations with complexity at most \(O(nd^2)\). Since \(d\) is typically small for tabular IoT data, the eigendecomposition overhead is negligible in practice. The threshold estimation step additionally scales linearly with \(n\).Therefore, the overall training complexity of XXX is upper bounded by:
\begin{equation}
O(ncdI + nd^2).
\label{eq:complexity_train_XXX_revised}
\end{equation}
For inference, projecting a test sample and computing distances to the \(c\) prototypes requires at most:
\begin{equation}
O\big(L(d+c)\big).
\label{eq:complexity_test_XXX_revised}
\end{equation}
The training memory complexity is bounded by:
\begin{equation}
O(nd+d^2),
\label{eq:complexity_memory_train_XXX}
\end{equation}
while the deployment memory footprint is:
\begin{equation}
O(dL+cL).
\label{eq:complexity_memory_test_XXX}
\end{equation}
Since inference is independent of the training size \(n\), XXX remains lightweight for IoT system deployment.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_su}

This section, we provide empirical validation for the three core structural challenges, we address three primary research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Detection Accuracy):} How does XXX compare against state-of-the-art anomaly detection baselines across diverse real-world IoT traffic benchmarks in one-class classification setting?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Robustness and Sensitivity):}  How robust is XXX under varying contamination levels and heterogeneous anomaly structures?
    \item \textbf{RQ3 (Resource Efficiency):} Does XXX maintain computational efficiency during both training and inference on large-scale tabular IoT datasets compared to representative anomaly detection baselines?
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Datasets and Evaluation Protocol}


For benchmarking in \textbf{RQ1} and \textbf{RQ2}, we construct the training sets by sampling up to $30{,}000$ benign instances per dataset, while the evaluation set is composed of one half attack and one half normal data, from six prominent public IoT security benchmarks (fully summarized in~\ref{ap1:datasets}): \textit{CICIoT2023}, \textit{ToN-IoT}, \textit{N-BaIoT}, \textit{BoT-IoT}, \textit{EdgeIIoTset}, and \textit{IoTID20}~\cite{neto2023ciciot2023,8490192,koroniotis2019towards,fesz-dm97-19}.

These six datasets are evaluated without any feature transformation to assess \textbf{RQ1} under real-world, cross-domain conditions. For \textbf{RQ2}, on each base IoT benchmark, we generate three distinct structural anomaly profiles, including \textit{local}, \textit{cluster-based}, and \textit{global}, synthesized via Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) perturbations following the ADBench setting, to evaluate susceptibility. Different slight levels of contamination (\emph{$1\%$, $3\%$, and $5\%$}) are introduced to describe imperfect supervision and test robustness in practical conditions (detail setting in~\ref{ap:anomaly_generation}).
                

For \textbf{RQ3}, besides conducting empirical evaluation of computational resource efficiency, including training and inference durations on the six datasets, we also perform additional analysis; specifically, a \textit{dimensional scalability analysis} is conducted on massively synthetically generated tabular data, with sample sizes expanding from $10^3$ to $10^6$ and feature dimensions ranging from $10^1$ to $10^4$.
 
\subsubsection{Baselines and Metrics}

We benchmark our proposed method against 22 representative anomaly detection algorithms (fully systematized and detailed in~\ref{ap1:ap_baselines}), categorized into seven distinct paradigms:
\textit{Proximity-based} methods, including KNN, LOF, and CBLOF;
\textit{Linear models}, such as PCA, OCSVM ;
\textit{Probabilistic and statistical} methods, comprising HBOS, ECOD, and COPOD;
\textit{Ensemble-based} approaches, such as IForest, LODA, SUOD, and DIF;
\textit{Hypersphere-based} deep models, including DeepSVDD, and DASVDD;
\textit{Reconstruction-based} models, such as AutoEncoder, VAE, and AE1SVM; and
\textit{Generative and representation learning} frameworks, including SO-GAAL, MO-GAAL, ALAD, LUNAR, and NeuTraLAD. All baseline models are implemented using the PyOD library \cite{zhao2019pyod} and evaluated under consistent experimental protocols to ensure a fair comparison. Detection performance is primarily quantified using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUC-ROC) and Precision–Recall curve (AUC-PR), alongside additional evaluation metrics. Detailed evaluation protocols are provided in~\ref{ap1:EM}.


\subsubsection{Preprocessing and Implementation Details}
Data preprocessing includes categorical encoding, duplicate removal, and numerical stabilization. We handle missing values and infinite residuals via median imputation and training-set mean substitution. All models are implemented in Python 3.12 using PyOD \cite{zhao2019pyod}, Scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011scikit} and \textbf{Pandas} for large-scale data manipulation. Experiments are conducted on a server equipped with an \textbf{AMD Ryzen 5 3600} 6-core processor and \textbf{64 GB of RAM}. The XXX algorithm is implemented using a custom linear NFST formulation with $k=75$ pseudo-classes. For full reproducibility, a detailed of the experimental environment and hyperparameter employed for all tested models is provided in~\ref{ap1:reproducibility}.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presented \textbf{XXX}, a lightweight one-class novelty detection framework for IoT intrusion detection under evolving open-world threat scenarios. XXX leverages a pseudo-class construction mechanism together with the compact representation property of the null space projection to preserve the underlying data structure while reducing sensitivity to imperfections in normal-only supervision. Furthermore, the framework employs prototype-based scoring in the projected space, enabling efficient inference by measuring distances to compact cluster representatives instead all training samples. Extensive experiments across six standard IoT benchmark datasets---CICIoT2023, ToN-IoT, N-BaIoT, BoT-IoT, EdgeIIoTset, and IoTID20---under diverse settings including varying contamination ratios and heterogeneous anomaly structures, demonstrate that XXX outperforms representative baselines. At the same time, it maintains low training and inference complexity, making it well-suited for edge deployment in IoT security environments.

\subsection{Dataset Splitting and Evaluation Protocol}
To rigorously and fairly evaluate anomaly detection performance across massive datasets while maintaining computational feasibility, we implement a tailored sub-sampling protocol to construct the training and evaluation sets:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Standard Protocol (CICIoT2023, N-BaIoT, ToN-IoT, EdgeIIoTset, IoTID20):} For these heavily populated datasets, we strictly sample $30{,}000$ normal instances to construct the benign-only training set. The evaluation set is explicitly balanced to avoid accuracy-masking in highly imbalanced domains, comprising exactly $15{,}000$ normal instances and $15{,}000$ anomaly instances sampled from the remaining data pool.
    \item \textbf{Exception Protocol (BoT-IoT):} BoT-IoT exhibits extreme natural class imbalance, yielding exactly $9{,}542$ normal samples across the entire raw dataset. To preserve natural distribution boundaries without applying oversampling techniques, we utilize approximately $7{,}000$ available normal instances for training. The evaluation set is constructed from the remaining data pool, resulting in a naturally imbalanced test set that authentically reflects the dataset's asymmetric threat landscape.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Protocols for Anomaly Synthesis and Training Contamination}
\label{ap:anomaly_generation}

To rigorously assess the boundary sensitivity and robustness of anomaly detection algorithms, we employ a dual-pronged evaluation protocol. This framework systematically explores two critical failure modes: (i) the detection of \textbf{structurally diverse anomalies} and (ii) resilience against \textbf{supervision noise} via training set contamination.

\subsection{Structural Anomaly Synthesis (Geometric Diversity)}
Following the benchmark protocols established in ADBench \cite{han2022adbench}, we perturb the benign data distribution to generate anomalies with distinct geometric characteristics. The underlying benign manifold is first modeled using a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) \cite{GMM}, where the number of components $n^* \in \{1, \dots, 5\}$ is selected by minimizing the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). From this manifold, we synthesize three structural anomaly profiles:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Local Anomalies (Density Perturbation)}: These model stealthy attacks that reside near benign regions but exhibit anomalous local densities. We preserve the mixture centers $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ but scale the fitted covariances by $\alpha = 5$: $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{new}} = \alpha \cdot \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{fitted}}$. This challenges models to distinguish between high-variance normal behavior and true anomalies.
    
    \item \textbf{Cluster Anomalies (Manifold Shift)}: These represent coordinated malicious activities (e.g., botnet clusters) that maintain internal correlations but exist in vacant feature space. We preserve the covariance structure $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ but shift the centroids: $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{new}} = \alpha \cdot \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{fitted}}$ (with $\alpha = 5$).
    
    \item \textbf{Global Anomalies (Uniform Noise)}: These simulate hardware failures or brute-force scanning. The manifold structure is discarded, and instances are sampled from a uniform distribution expanded $10\%$ beyond the extreme bounds of each feature dimension $X_k$:
    $x_k \sim \text{Uniform}(\min(X_k) \times 1.1, \max(X_k) \times 1.1)$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Supervision Noise Protocol (Training Contamination)}
\label{ap:noise_contamination}

In operational environments, obtaining a purely "clean" training set is often infeasible. To evaluate performance under imperfect supervision, we implement a \textbf{Noise Contamination Protocol} where semantically diverse anomalies (real-world attacks) are injected into the training corpus at varying contamination rates $\rho \in \{1\%, 3\%, 5\%\}$.

Let $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ be the initial benign set and $\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}^{+}$ be the set of available anomalies. For a specific rate $\rho$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sampling}: We randomly sample $n_{\text{noise}} = \lfloor |\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}| \cdot \rho \rfloor$ instances from $\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}^{+}$ to form a contamination set $\mathcal{A}_{\text{inject}}$. Due to the diverse nature of $\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}^{+}$, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{inject}}$ inherently contains varied attack types (e.g., DDoS, Mirai, Scanning).
    \item \textbf{Isolation}: To prevent data leakage, instances in $\mathcal{A}_{\text{inject}}$ are strictly excluded from the evaluation test set.
    \item \textbf{Poisoning}: The training set is modified as $\mathcal{D}'_{\text{train}} = \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} \cup \mathcal{A}_{\text{inject}}$, where poisoned samples are mislabeled as "normal" (label 0). 
\end{enumerate}


\section{Baseline Algorithms Systematization}
\label{ap1:ap_baselines}

To ensure a comprehensive and fair comparison, we evaluate our proposed method against 22 baseline models, comprehensively divided into classical \textbf{Shallow Learning} structures and representation-heavy \textbf{Deep Learning} frameworks.

\subsection{Shallow Learning Approaches}
We consider four major families of classical unsupervised anomaly detection methods:

\textbf{1. Proximity-based Methods:} 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)} \cite{KNN}: Computes anomaly scores based on the exact continuous distance to the $k$-th closest neighbor point.
    \item \textit{Local Outlier Factor (LOF)} \cite{LOF}: Identifies anomalous data points by measuring the local density deviation of a given data point with respect to its neighbors. 
    \item \textit{Clustering-Based Local Outlier Factor (CBLOF)} \cite{CBLOF}: Speeds up processing by grouping data into dominant functional clusters first, scoring anomalies primarily via their Euclidean distance to macro-cluster centroids.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{2. Linear Models:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Principal Component Analysis (PCA)} \cite{PCA}: A traditional dimensional reduction technique where outliers are detected via a determine reconstruction errors threshold from the reduced principal components.
    \item \textit{One-Class Support Vector Machine (OCSVM)} \cite{OCSVM}: Constructs an optimal non-linear hypersphere boundary, trained purely on normal data, explicitly cordoning off the likely regions of anomaly injection.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{3. Probabilistic and Statistical Methods:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Histogram-Based Outlier Detection (HBOS)} \cite{HBOS}: Aggregates deterministic univariate anomaly propensities, operating under feature independence assumptions for rapid high-speed execution profiles.
    \item \textit{Empirical Cumulative Distribution-Based Outlier Detection (ECOD)} \cite{ECOD}:Detects outliers based on the empirical cumulative distribution tails, trapping anomalies without pre-configured parametric distribution assumptions.
    \item \textit{Copula-Based Outlier Detector (COPOD)} \cite{COPOD}: Uses multivariate cumulative distribution functions via parameterized empirical copulas to interpret interdependent anomalies.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{4. Ensemble-based Approaches:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Isolation Forest (IForest)} \cite{IForest}: Identifies anomalies by tracking iterative path lengths in randomized tree partitions.
    \item \textit{Lightweight Online Detector of Anomalies (LODA)} \cite{LODA}: Executes a rapid ensemble of lightweight one-dimensional histograms projected via random vectors.
    \item \textit{Scalable Unsupervised Outlier Detection (SUOD)} \cite{zhao2020suod}: A framework that accelerates large-scale ensemble execution through optimized parallelization and model selection.
    \item \textit{Deep Isolation Forest (DIF)} \cite{DIF}: Evolves the IForest paradigm by mapping the original data through neural network representations before computing algorithmic isolation paths.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Deep Learning Frameworks}
\textbf{1. Hypersphere-based Deep Models:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Deep Support Vector Data Description (DeepSVDD)} \cite{DeepSVDD}: Trains a deep neural network to map normal data into a compact hypersphere in the latent space.
    \item \textit{Dual-Autoencoder SVDD (DASVDD)} \cite{DASVDD}: Enhances DeepSVDD with a dual autoencoder structure, auto-encoding topological penalty to mitigate hypersphere collapse.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{2. Reconstruction-based Models:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{AutoEncoder} \cite{Autoencoder}: A type of artificial neural network, detects anomalies through high reconstruction error after training on benign data only.     
    \item \textit{Variational Autoencoder (VAE)} \cite{VAE}: Extends the standard autoencoder with a probabilistic latent space regularized by KL-divergence, scoring anomalies based on reconstruction probability.
    \item \textit{Autoencoder-based One-Class SVM (AE1SVM)} \cite{AE1SVM}: Combines the representation power of an autoencoder with the strong decision boundary of OC-SVM applied in the learned latent space.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{3. Generative and Representation Models:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Single/Multi-Objective Generative Adversarial Active Learning}( \textit{SO-GAAL} \& \textit{MO-GAAL}) \cite{SOGAAL}: Employ single and multiple Generator-Discriminator pairs within Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to synthesize pseudo-anomalies, thereby enhancing the discriminator’s ability to distinguish between normal and anomalous regions.
    \item \textit{Adversarial Learned Anomaly Detection (ALAD)} \cite{ALAD}: Employs bi-directional GANs to minimize reconstruction discrepancies between original and reconstructed samples in both data and latent spaces.
    \item \textit{Neural Transformation Learning for Anomaly Detection (NeuTraL)}~\cite{qiu2021neural}: Employs self-supervised learning by training the network to predict the type of geometric or statistical transformations applied to the input data. Since anomalies deviate from the normal data manifold, they produce inconsistent predictions under these transformations, enabling effective anomaly scoring.
    \item \textit{Learning Unsupervised Anomaly Representations with Neural Networks (LUNAR)}~\cite{lunar}: Learns a neural embedding of the data and constructs a k-nearest neighbor graph in the embedding space. A graph neural network with message passing is then employed to learn rich local topological representations. Anomalies are identified based on their deviation from the aggregated neighborhood structures.
\end{itemize}

\section{Evaluation Metrics Formulation}
\label{ap1:EM}

To systematically benchmark the anomaly detection models, we focus our reporting strategy on metrics tailored for the unique challenges of IoT network traffic analysis. In operational IoT environments, the dataset distribution is naturally characterized by extreme class imbalance: benign background traffic massively overwhelms the sporadic instances of anomalous or hostile activity. Under such conditions, standard threshold-dependent metrics like Accuracy provide an overly optimistic evaluation of model performance because they are dominated by True Negatives. To provide a rigorous and threshold-independent assessment of detection capabilities, we establish the \textbf{Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC)} and the \textbf{Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUC-PR)} as our primary evaluation criteria.

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{AUC-ROC} quantifies the general ability of the model to rank true anomalies higher than benign samples across all possible decision thresholds. It serves as a strong baseline indicator of overall discrimination capacity.
    \[ \text{AUC-ROC} = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \frac{\text{TPR}_{i+1} + \text{TPR}_i}{2} \cdot (\text{FPR}_{i+1} - \text{FPR}_i) \]
    
    \item \textbf{AUC-PR} is strictly prioritized as the definitive performance validator for our experiments. Unlike AUC-ROC, which factors in True Negatives (and is thus inflated by the massive volume of benign IoT traffic), AUC-PR focuses exclusively on the positive (anomalous) class. It directly evaluates the critical operational trade-off between accurately identifying sparse network threats (Recall) and minimizing false alarms (Precision) without being masked by baseline class distributions.
    \[ \text{AUC-PR} = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} (\text{Recall}_{i+1} - \text{Recall}_i) \cdot \frac{\text{Precision}_{i+1} + \text{Precision}_i}{2} \]
\end{itemize}

Additional discrete metrics, including Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score, are reported to provide a comprehensive operational profile of the models at a dynamically calibrated decision threshold.

\begin{itemize}    
    \item \textbf{Accuracy (ACC):} The global proportion of flawlessly predicted classifications.
    \[ \text{ACC} = \frac{\text{TP} + \text{TN}}{\text{TP} + \text{TN} + \text{FP} + \text{FN}} \]
    
    \item \textbf{Recall / True Positive Rate (TPR):} Measures the model's ability to correctly identify actual anomalies (attacks) out of all real attack instances.
    \[ \text{TPR} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}} \]
    
    \item \textbf{False Positive Rate (FPR):} The absolute fractional severity of noise continually generated by incorrectly triggered false alarms.
    \[ \text{FPR} = \frac{\text{FP}}{\text{FP} + \text{TN}} \]
    
    \item \textbf{Precision (PPV)}: Measures the proportion of correctly identified anomalies among all instances predicted as anomalies. High precision indicates low false alarm rates.
    \[ \text{PPV} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}} \]
    
    \item \textbf{F1-score}: The harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, providing a balanced measure of detection performance when both precision and recall are important.
    \[ \text{F1} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{TPR} \cdot \text{PPV}}{\text{TPR} + \text{PPV}} \]
    % \item \textbf{Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)}: A balanced metric that measures the correlation between predicted and actual labels, remaining reliable even under severe class imbalance.
    % \[ \text{MCC} = \frac{\text{TP} \cdot \text{TN} - \text{FP} \cdot \text{FN}}{\sqrt{(\text{TP} + \text{FP})(\text{TP} + \text{FN})(\text{TN} + \text{FP})(\text{TN} + \text{FN})}} \]

\subsubsection{Comparison with State-of-the-Art}

To comprehensively assess the effectiveness of our proposed method, \textbf{XXX}, we compare its performance against results synthesized from several recent state-of-the-art approaches published from 2022 onward. While the baseline metrics were extracted directly from their respective original publications to ensure an objective overview, the XXX values were obtained through our own experimental suite on a local server using the standardized protocol defined in Section~\ref{sec:exp_su}. These methods span a range of machine learning and deep learning techniques applied to IoT intrusion detection, including optimization-based models~\cite{aktar2024robust}, genetic algorithms~\cite{saiyed2024genetic}, ensemble frameworks~\cite{golestani2024device}, and unsupervised learning techniques~\cite{segurola2024unsupervised}. The evaluation is conducted on four widely-used public datasets: \textbf{N-BaIoT}, \textbf{ToN-IoT}, \textbf{CICIoT2023}, and \textbf{BoT-IoT}.


Table~\ref{tab:ids_comparison} reports the performance of all methods in terms of standard classification metrics: \textbf{Accuracy (ACC)}, \textbf{Precision (PRE)}, \textbf{Recall (REC)}, and \textbf{F1-score (F1)}. Our method, XXX, demonstrates competitive performance across the evaluated datasets.

On the \textbf{N-BaIoT} and \textbf{ToN-IoT} datasets, XXX achieves F1-scores of 96.44\% and 99.15\%, respectively, aligning with the top-tier performance reported by recent supervised and semi-supervised works. Notably, XXX maintains balanced precision (99.44\%) and recall (98.87\%) on ToN-IoT, indicating consistent detection capabilities without dataset-specific bias.

In the more challenging \textbf{CICIoT2023} and \textbf{BoT-IoT} datasets, which feature highly complex and imbalanced traffic patterns, the structural design of XXX prioritizes threat sensitivity. On BoT-IoT, the model achieves a strong recall of 96.47\%, though precision drops to 72.12\% (F1-score: 82.54\%). Similarly, on CICIoT2023, XXX delivers a high recall of 95.68\% alongside an 88.66\% precision. However, in mission-critical IoT environments, minimizing False Negatives (missed intrusions) via high recall is paramount, as a single undetected breach carries far more severe consequences than investigating a false alarm.

In summary, the empirical results indicate that XXX provides a balanced detection capability across different IoT datasets. By effectively capturing the local structure of normal traffic, the framework maintains high recall rates, which is a necessary characteristic for practical anomaly detection systems aiming to minimize missed intrusions.
