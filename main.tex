\section{Introduction}

While the Internet of Things (IoT) is widely deployed across domains such as healthcare, smart homes, and industrial automation, securing these systems remains challenging due to heterogeneous and resource-constrained edge devices. Therefore, intrusion detection systems (IDS) are widely deployed to monitor network activity and detect potential threats. However, traditional signature-based IDS primarily detect known attack patterns and are ineffective against attacks that mimic benign traffic or rapidly evolve~\cite{DiazVerdejo2022SIDS}. This limitation is further exacerbated by generative technologies such as Large Language Models (LLMs), which significantly reduce the effort required to generate exploit scripts, malware variants, and adaptive attack strategies~\cite{Ferrag2025GenAICybersec,Xu2025LLMAutonomousAttack}. Consequently, attack behaviors may evolve faster than security datasets can be curated and labeled. Although machine learning (ML)-based IDS attempt to learn detection rules from labeled data, most supervised approaches implicitly assume a closed-set setting and tend to overfit known attack categories, resulting in reduced reliability when encountering emerging or zero-day attacks absent from training data~\cite{Fuhrman2025CNDIDS,Zhang2020OpenSet}.


Under an open-world threat model, intrusion detection is more appropriately framed as \emph{one-class novelty detection} (OCND), where models trained exclusively on benign traffic identify potential intrusions as deviations from learned normal behavior. This formulation aligns with practical deployments, where benign traffic is easier to collect with high confidence than obtaining comprehensive attack labels.

\subsection{One-Class Novelty Detection for IoT Intrusion Detection and Its Challenges}

Although OCND aligns with the open-world nature of IoT security, its practical reliability is shaped by several structural challenges common in IoT environments, including contaminated benign training data, heterogeneous anomaly structures, and strict edge resource constraints. We therefore focus on three key challenges.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Imperfect benign supervision.} 
In one-class pipelines, the benign training set can include undetected attacks or mislabeled samples due to asymmetric annotation errors, where malicious traffic is labeled as normal. Even low contamination rates (e.g., 0.3\%) can distort the decision boundary and reduce detection reliability~\cite{Prabowo2026UncleanTrainingNIDS}.

\item \textbf{Structurally diverse anomaly manifestations.} 
IoT attacks do not follow a single deviation pattern. They can appear as global distributional shifts, cluster-level deviations, or subtle local anomalies. A single global threshold or representation captures these structures poorly, which weakens conventional OCND assumptions~\cite{han2022adbench,KANG2019772}.

\item \textbf{Constrained edge deployment regimes.} 
IoT IDS must operate under strict latency, memory, and computational constraints at the network edge. These constraints favor lightweight models with CPU-efficient inference and compact memory footprints, limiting the practicality of heavyweight deep architectures and sharpening the trade-off between robustness and efficiency~\cite{Rahman2025IoTIDSSurvey, beggel2019robust,jodelka2021adaptive}.
\end{itemize}

Existing anomaly-detection approaches typically address only a subset of these requirements. Methods designed to capture diverse anomaly structures are often sensitive to contaminated training data~\cite{perera2019ocgan,gardner2006one,han2022adbench}. In contrast, approaches that explicitly model training-set noise usually introduce additional complexity that limits their practicality for resource-constrained IoT edge deployment~\cite{beggel2019robust,jodelka2021adaptive,Rahman2025IoTIDSSurvey}.As a result, OCND frameworks that jointly address anomaly diversity, contamination robustness, and computational efficiency remain limited in the IoT IDS literature (see Section~\ref{sec:method} for further discussion).


To address this gap, we propose \textbf{XXX}, a one-class reformulation of the Null Foley--Sammon Transform (NFST)~\cite{Guo2006} for IoT intrusion detection under slight benign-set contamination and heterogeneous normal traffic structures. XXX serves as a lightweight, structure-aware projection layer that preserves the computational efficiency of NFST while adapting it to these deployment conditions. To the best of our knowledge, this work is among the first to explicitly study IoT intrusion detection under an open-set setting and formulate it as a benign-only OCND problem.

\subsection{Proposed Approach and Solution}
The Null Foley–Sammon Transformation (NFST)~\cite{Guo2006} learns a discriminant projection by maximizing the Fisher criterion in the null space of the within-class scatter matrix, achieving theoretically infinite class separability. Because NFST operates through a linear projection learned from the data distribution, it can capture structural relationships in the data while maintaining low computational overhead at inference time. These properties make NFST well suited for OCND, where the objective is to model the structure of normal data and identify deviations from it. NFST has shown strong performance in supervised computer vision tasks~\cite{ayad2024efficient,perera2021one} and has recently been explored for multiclass novelty detection in intrusion detection systems~\cite{nguyen2025nnfst}.

We present \textbf{XXX} as a one-class reformulation of NFST for IoT IDS. The framework addresses the preceding challenges through two design principles:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Pseudo multi-class construction for imperfect benign supervision.}
Instead of treating benign traffic as a single homogeneous class, XXX partitions it into pseudo-classes to expose latent substructure in normal behavior. This reduces the influence of contaminated samples because anomalous regions are less likely to dominate any single pseudo-class.

\item \textbf{Localized cluster-aware scoring for heterogeneous anomaly structures.}
Instead of relying on a single global decision boundary, XXX evaluates deviations relative to local behavioral regimes defined by benign clusters. This localized scoring improves sensitivity to heterogeneous anomaly patterns, including global shifts, cluster-level deviations, and subtle local outliers.

\end{itemize}

The resulting model retains a lightweight structure because inference only requires a simple NFST-derived linear projection, making it suitable for latency- and resource-constrained IoT edge environments.

However, adapting NFST directly to one-class novelty detection in IoT environments is non-trivial, particularly in the large-sample tabular regime common in network traffic datasets where $n \gg d$. Classical NFST is primarily motivated by the small-sample-size (SSS) setting, whereas outside this regime the existence and discriminative utility of null-space solutions depend on additional rank conditions rather than being guaranteed by construction~\cite{ali2022theoretical}. Consequently, a naive application of NFST to large tabular IoT datasets may produce degenerate or weakly informative projections~\cite{nguyen2025nnfst}. Two issues must therefore be addressed to enable NFST to operate reliably under the $n \gg d$ regime.

\textbf{(1) Theoretical degeneracy under a single-class formulation.}
In a naive one-class setting, all training samples belong to a single benign class, collapsing the scatter structure required by NFST and leading to degenerate null-space solutions. To address this issue, we introduce a data-driven localization mechanism that partitions benign traffic into pseudo-classes, restoring the structural variability required by NFST.

\textbf{(2) Numerical instability in null-space construction.}
Classical NFST implementations typically rely on Gram--Schmidt orthonormalization to construct the null-space projection basis. However, in the $n \gg d$ regime common in IoT traffic datasets, this procedure became numerically unstable due to highly correlated tabular features and failed to produce a valid orthonormal projection basis in our experiments (see Section~\ref{sec:exp_r&d}). This phenomenon has not been discussed in prior NFST-based work. We therefore employ Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) for null-space construction, which provides numerically robust orthonormal bases and enables stable NFST computation.


\subsection{Main Contributions}
In summary, the main contributions of this research are as follows:
    
\begin{itemize}

\item We reformulate open-world IoT intrusion detection as a one-class novelty detection (OCND) problem under imperfect benign supervision, heterogeneous anomaly structures, and edge resource constraints. Based on this formulation, we propose a unified solution combining pseudo multi-class construction and localized cluster-aware anomaly scoring (see Section~\ref{sec:method}).


\item We propose \textbf{XXX}, a lightweight NFST-based anomaly detection framework for IoT traffic. We identify a previously overlooked numerical instability in classical NFST implementations based on Gram--Schmidt orthonormalization when applied to large tabular datasets ($n \gg d$), and replace it with Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) for stable null-space construction, enabling reliable linear projections suitable for edge deployment.


\item Experiments on three standard IoT benchmarks (CICIoT2023, ToN-IoT, and BoT-IoT~\cite{neto2023ciciot2023,8490192,koroniotis2019towards,fesz-dm97-19}), together with three synthetic anomaly types (local, cluster, and global) and annotation-noise settings that emulate benign-set contamination, demonstrate the effectiveness of XXX (see Section~\ref{sec:exp_r&d}).
\end{itemize}

Intrusion Detection Systems (IDSs) are a fundamental component of securing Internet of Things (IoT) networks against cyber threats such as distributed denial-of-service attacks, botnet propagation, and probing activities. Due to the scale and heterogeneity of IoT environments, anomaly detection has become a widely adopted paradigm for identifying malicious traffic patterns that deviate from normal network behavior~\cite{samara2022survey}. Unlike signature-based approaches that rely on predefined attack patterns, anomaly detection methods aim to learn the characteristics of benign traffic and detect deviations that may indicate previously unseen attacks.

\subsection{Anomaly Detection for IoT Intrusion Detection}

Early machine learning approaches to anomaly detection in network security largely rely on shallow models that identify abnormal observations based on geometric or statistical properties of the data distribution. These methods include distance-based and density-based techniques that detect samples with low local density or large distances from their neighbors, as well as statistical models that estimate the underlying distribution of normal traffic. Ensemble-based algorithms such as isolation-based methods have also been widely adopted due to their scalability and effectiveness in high-dimensional settings~\cite{IForest,goldstein2016comparative}. 

These classical approaches offer several advantages for IoT deployments, including relatively low computational cost and interpretable detection mechanisms. However, their effectiveness may degrade when network traffic exhibits complex nonlinear relationships or heterogeneous anomaly structures. In particular, many shallow models rely on simplified assumptions about feature independence or global density patterns, which may not hold in realistic IoT environments where traffic patterns are highly dynamic.

To address these limitations, recent studies have explored deep learning techniques for anomaly detection in network traffic. Representation learning models aim to capture complex patterns in high-dimensional data through nonlinear feature transformations. Reconstruction-based architectures such as autoencoders detect anomalies by measuring reconstruction errors, while hypersphere-based models attempt to learn compact representations of normal behavior in latent space~\cite{DeepSVDD}. Generative models based on adversarial learning have also been investigated to improve anomaly detection by synthesizing pseudo-outliers or learning robust representations of normal traffic patterns~\cite{SOGAAL,ALAD}. Although these approaches often achieve strong empirical performance, they typically require large training datasets and significant computational resources, which may limit their deployment in resource-constrained IoT systems.

\subsection{One-Class Novelty Detection}

Given the open-world nature of cybersecurity threats, intrusion detection is often formulated as a one-class novelty detection (OCND) problem, where models are trained primarily on benign data and anomalies are detected as deviations from learned normal behavior. Classical OCND approaches include boundary-based methods such as One-Class SVM, reconstruction-based models such as autoencoders, and hypersphere-based frameworks such as DeepSVDD~\cite{shin2005one,DeepSVDD}. However, most OCND models assume that the training data represent clean normal behavior and that anomalies can be detected through global deviations from the learned distribution; in practical IoT deployments, this assumption is often violated due to label noise, undetected attacks embedded in benign datasets, and heterogeneous anomaly structures in network traffic~\cite{han2022adbench,Rahman2025IoTIDSSurve}.In addition, many OCND approaches rely on computationally expensive optimization procedures or deep architectures. For example, kernel-based methods such as One-Class SVM can exhibit cubic training complexity with respect to the number of samples, which leads to significant computational overhead and limits their deployment in resource-constrained IoT environments~\cite{shin2005one}.





\subsection{Robust Anomaly Detection under Imperfect Data}

Recent work has therefore focused on improving the robustness of anomaly detection models under imperfect supervision. Several approaches attempt to mitigate the impact of contaminated training data through robust objective functions, adversarial learning strategies, or iterative filtering of suspected outliers during training~\cite{perera2019ocgan,beggel2019robust}. These techniques aim to reduce the influence of anomalous samples when learning the representation of normal data.

Despite these advances, many robust anomaly detection frameworks rely on complex deep architectures or computationally intensive training procedures. Such requirements may limit their applicability in IoT environments, where intrusion detection systems must operate under strict resource constraints and process large volumes of streaming traffic in real time.

These limitations highlight the need for anomaly detection approaches that combine robustness to imperfect training data with computational efficiency suitable for practical IoT deployments.



\subsection{One-Class Novelty Detection}

Given the open-world nature of cybersecurity threats, intrusion detection has increasingly been formulated as a one-class novelty detection (OCND) problem, where models are trained exclusively on benign data and anomalies are detected as deviations from learned normal behavior. Classical OCND methods include boundary-based approaches such as \textbf{One-Class SVM}, reconstruction-based models such as autoencoders, and hypersphere-based frameworks including DeepSVDD~\cite{shin2005one,DeepSVDD}. These models are particularly attractive in security applications because benign traffic is typically easier to collect than comprehensive labeled attack datasets.

However, most OCND approaches assume that the training data accurately represent clean normal behavior and that anomalies can be identified through global deviations from the learned distribution. In practical IoT deployments, these assumptions are often violated due to label noise, undetected attacks within benign datasets, and heterogeneous anomaly structures that may manifest at multiple levels of the data distribution.

\subsection{Robust Anomaly Detection under Imperfect Supervision}

Several studies have investigated anomaly detection methods that remain robust under imperfect supervision or contaminated training data. Robust learning frameworks attempt to mitigate the influence of anomalous samples during training through techniques such as robust objective functions, outlier filtering, or adversarial training strategies~\cite{perera2019ocgan,beggel2019robust}. While these approaches improve resilience to label noise, many rely on complex deep architectures or iterative optimization procedures that increase computational cost.

For IoT intrusion detection systems deployed at the network edge, computational efficiency and low memory usage are critical requirements. Methods that rely on heavy neural architectures or expensive training procedures may therefore be impractical in real-world deployments~\cite{Rahman2025IoTIDSSurvey,jodelka2021adaptive}. Consequently, developing anomaly detection frameworks that simultaneously address heterogeneous anomaly structures, contaminated benign training data, and resource-constrained deployment conditions remains an open challenge.

These limitations motivate the development of lightweight yet robust anomaly detection methods that can operate effectively under imperfect supervision in IoT environments.

\subsection{Problem Definition: Robust One-Class Novelty Detection}
\label{sec:problem_def}

Consider an IoT network monitoring environment where the feature space is denoted by $\mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$.   In the standard One-Class Novelty Detection (OCND) setting, we represent the normal traffic data as random variables drawn from an underlying probability distribution $\mathbb{P}_{norm}$ over $\mathcal{X}$. We are given a training dataset $\mathcal{D}_{train} = \{x_1, \ldots, x_N\}$, where $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$.

The fundamental goal of OCND is to learn a scoring function $S: \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ (or a decision boundary) that captures the support of $\mathbb{P}_{norm}$. During the inference phase, for a query sample $x_{test}$, the decision function $f(x_{test})$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    f(x_{test}) = \begin{cases} 
    0 (\text{Normal}) & \text{if } S(x_{test}) \leq \tau \\
    1 (\text{Anomaly}) & \text{if } S(x_{test}) > \tau 
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:ocnd_decision}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is a decision threshold determined to satisfy a target false positive rate (FPR) on the training set. Ideally, $S(x)$ assigns lower values to samples generated by $\mathbb{P}_{norm}$ and higher values to samples from any anomalous distribution $\mathbb{P}_{anom}$. In the context of IoT intrusion detection, this task faces three fundamental challenges which we formulate as follows:

\textbf{1. Annotation Errors (Noisy Training Data):}
Unlike ideal One-Class settings where the training set $\mathcal{D}_{train}$ is pure, real-world IoT datasets often contain mislabeled anomalies due to the sheer volume of traffic ~\cite{xu2023deep, han2022adbench}. We model $\mathcal{D}_{train}$ as a contaminated set of $N$ samples:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_{train} = \mathcal{X}_{norm} \cup \mathcal{X}_{noise}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{X}_{norm}$ represents true normal patterns and $\mathcal{X}_{noise}$ denotes unknown anomalies (annotation errors), with the assumption that $|\mathcal{X}_{noise}| \ll |\mathcal{X}_{norm}|$. The challenge is to learn the support of $\mathcal{X}_{norm}$ while robustly ignoring $\mathcal{X}_{noise}$.

\textbf{2. Diverse Anomaly Types (Multi-modal Distribution):}
We reject the simplifying assumption that normal IoT traffic follows a single unimodal distribution \cite{steinbuss2021benchmarking}. Instead, drawing upon the taxonomy of anomalies defined in the comprehensive ADBench benchmark\cite{han2022adbench}, we posit that the normal feature space $\mathcal{X}_{norm}$ exhibits a \textbf{multi-modal structure} arising from heterogeneous protocols and device behaviors. Mathematically, $\mathcal{X}_{norm}$ is drawn from a mixture of $K$ latent sub-distributions (e.g., Gaussian Mixture Model):
\begin{equation}
    x \sim \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \mathcal{P}_k(x|\mu_k, \Sigma_k)
\end{equation}
where $\mu_k$ and $\Sigma_k$ denote the mean and covariance of the $k$-th semantic cluster (e.g., HTTP, MQTT streams). To be robust, the detection mechanism must account for specific anomaly types as formalized in ADBench:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Local Anomalies:} Instances that fall within the global data range but deviate from the local covariance structure ($\Sigma_k$) of their specific neighborhood cluster $\mathcal{P}_k$. In IoT, these represent subtle deviations like slow-rate probing.
    \item \textbf{Clustered (Group) Anomalies:} Anomalies that exhibit similar characteristics and form dense groups, modeled as distributions with shifted means ($\mu' = \alpha \mu_k$). These correspond to synchronized botnet activities where compromised devices behave similarly to each other but differently from normal clusters.
    \item \textbf{Global Anomalies:} Instances generated from a uniform distribution far outside the support of any normal cluster $\mathcal{P}_k$, representing high-intensity attacks like volumetric DDoS.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{ Null Foley-Sammon Transformation briefly and its weakness}


The Null Foley-Sammon Transform (NFST), introduced by Guo et al.~\cite{Guo2006}, is a discriminative linear projection technique designed to maximize class separability based on the Fisher discriminant criterion. NFST maps all samples of a given class to a single point in a discriminative nullspace, achieving zero within-class variance while maintaining positive between-class variance. This section provides a detailed mathematical formulation of NFST, its key properties, and its limitations, particularly in the small sample size (SSS) case where the number of training samples \( n \) is less than the feature dimension \( d \).


Consider a dataset with \( n \) samples \( \{\mathbf{x}_k\}_{k=1}^n \in \mathbb{R}^d \), belonging to \( c \) classes, where the \( i \)-th class has \( n_i \) samples with class mean \( \mathbf{m}_i \), and \( \mathbf{m} \) is the overall mean of the dataset. For a sample \( \mathbf{x}_k \) in the \( j \)-th class, define the centered sample as \( \overline{\mathbf{x}}_k = \mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{m}_j \). The within-class scatter matrix \( \mathbf{S}_w \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} \) and between-class scatter matrix \( \mathbf{S}_b \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} \) are defined as:
\[
\mathbf{S}_w = \sum_{k=1}^n \overline{\mathbf{x}}_k \overline{\mathbf{x}}_k^\top, \quad \mathbf{S}_b = \sum_{i=1}^c n_i (\mathbf{m}_i - \mathbf{m})(\mathbf{m}_i - \mathbf{m})^\top.
\]
The total scatter matrix is given by \( \mathbf{S}_t = \mathbf{S}_w + \mathbf{S}_b \).

The Foley-Sammon Transform (FST) ~\cite{Foley1975} seeks an optimal set of orthonormal discriminant vectors \( \mathbf{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d \) that maximize the Fisher criterion:
% \[
% J(\mathbf{w}_i) = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{S}_b \mathbf{w}_i}{\mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{w}_i}, \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{w}_j = \delta_{ij}, \quad j = 1, \ldots, i-1,
% \]

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
J(\mathbf{w}_i) &= \frac{\mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{S}_b \mathbf{w}_i}{\mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{w}_i} \\
\text{subject to} \quad & \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{w}_j = \delta_{ij}, \quad j = 1, \ldots, i-1
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:FisherCri}
\end{equation}


where \( \delta_{ij} \) is the Kronecker delta. The NFST extends FST by utilizing the nullspace of \( \mathbf{S}_w \). Specifically, NFST seeks discriminant vectors \( \mathbf{w} \), termed Null Projecting Directions (NPDs), where the numerator and denominator in Eq.\eqref{eq:FisherCri} satisfy: 
\[
\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{w} = 0, \quad \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{S}_b \mathbf{w} > 0.
\]
Since \( \mathbf{S}_w \) is positive semi-definite, the condition \( \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{w} = 0 \) implies \( \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0} \) (by Lemma 1 in~\cite{Ali2023}), meaning \( \mathbf{w} \) lies in the nullspace of \( \mathbf{S}_w \), denoted:
\[
\mathbf{N}_w = \{\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^d \mid \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\}.
\]
The condition \( \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{S}_b \mathbf{w} > 0 \) ensures discriminative capability, equivalent to \( \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{S}_t \mathbf{w} > 0 \), as \( \mathbf{S}_t = \mathbf{S}_w + \mathbf{S}_b \). Thus, NPDs lie in the intersection of \( \mathbf{N}_w \) and the orthogonal complement of the nullspace of \( \mathbf{S}_t \), i.e., \( \mathbf{N}_t^\perp \), where:
\[
\mathbf{N}_t = \{\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^d \mid \mathbf{S}_t \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\}.
\]
The projection matrix \( \mathbf{W} = [\mathbf{w}_1, \mathbf{w}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{w}_L] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times L} \) is formed by an orthonormal basis of subspace \( \mathbf{N}_t^\perp \cap \mathbf{N}_w \), and the NFST is defined as:
\[
\mathbf{y}_k = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x}_k.
\]
This transformation ensures that all samples of a class collapse to a single point in the discriminative nullspace, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:cluster_impact} (adapted from~\cite{Ali2023}).



To compute the NPDs, let \( \mathbf{X}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \) contain the zero-mean centered data \( \{\mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{m}\}_{k=1}^n \). The rowspace of \( \mathbf{S}_t = \mathbf{X}_t \mathbf{X}_t^\top \) is the column space of \( \mathbf{X}_t \). Assuming \( \mathbf{X}_t \) has rank \( r \), Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization on \( \{\mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{m}\}_{k=1}^n \) yields an orthonormal basis \( \mathbf{Q} = [\mathbf{q}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{q}_r] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r} \) for \( \mathbf{N}_t^\perp \). A vector \( \mathbf{w} \in \mathbf{N}_t^\perp \) can be expressed as:
\[
\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{Q} \boldsymbol{\beta}, \quad \boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^r.
\]
For \( \mathbf{w} \in \mathbf{N}_w \), we require \( \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{Q} \boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{0} \), which implies:
\[
\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{Q} \boldsymbol{\beta} = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{Q} \boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{0}.
\] 
Using Lemma 1 in~\cite{Ali2023}. The nullspace of \( \mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{Q} \), with dimension \( L \), provides orthonormal vectors \( \boldsymbol{\beta}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{\beta}_L \). The NPDs are then \( \mathbf{w}_i = \mathbf{Q} \boldsymbol{\beta}_i \), and the projection matrix is:
\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{B}, \quad \mathbf{B} = [\boldsymbol{\beta}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{\beta}_L] \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times L}.
\]
The standard algorithm (s-NFST) for computing NFST is summarized in Algorithm ~\ref{alg:s-nfst} ~\cite{Ali2023}.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{s-NFST Algorithm}
\label{alg:s-nfst}
\begin{algorithmic}
\REQUIRE Data \( \mathbf{X} = [\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \) with labels
\ENSURE Projection matrix \( \mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times L} \), mapped data \( \mathbf{Y} = [\mathbf{y}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{y}_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times n} \)
\STATE Compute \( \mathbf{S}_w \) using \( \mathbf{X} \) and labels
\STATE \( \mathbf{Q} \gets \) Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization on \( \{\mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{m}\}_{k=1}^n \)
\STATE \( \mathbf{B} \gets \) Nullspace basis of \( \mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{Q} \)
\STATE \( \mathbf{W} = \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{B} \), \( \mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{X} \)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}




In the s-NFST algorithm, \( \mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r} \) is an orthonormal basis for the column space of the zero-mean centered data matrix \( \mathbf{X}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \), where \( r = \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}_t) \leq \min(n, d) = d \) (typically \( r = d \) in the LSS case under Assumption 1(b) ~\cite{Ali2023}). Gram-Schmidt orthonormalizes the columns of \( \mathbf{X}_t \), but numerical instability can produce an ill-defined \( \mathbf{Q} \) (e.g., non-orthogonal or near-zero columns), disrupting the nullspace computation of \( \mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{Q} \). 

For \( \mathbf{Q} \) to be ill-defined (interpreted as numerically invalid, akin to ``\( \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{0} \)'') while \( \mathbf{U}_r \neq \mathbf{0} \), \( \mathbf{X}_t \neq \mathbf{0} \) (i.e., \( r \geq 1 \)) is required, as \( \mathbf{X}_t = \mathbf{0} \) would imply both \( \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{0} \) and \( \mathbf{U}_r = \mathbf{0} \) (no column space). The following scenarios in the LSS case (\( n > d \)) can cause Gram-Schmidt to fail:

\begin{definition}[Highly Collinear Data]
If the columns of \( \mathbf{X}_t \) are nearly linearly dependent (high condition number, i.e., ratio of largest to smallest non-zero singular value is large), Gram-Schmidt accumulates numerical errors, producing a \( \mathbf{Q} \) with non-orthogonal or near-zero columns. SVD computes \( \mathbf{U}_r \) accurately, as it explicitly handles small singular values, ensuring a valid basis.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Ill-Conditioned \( \mathbf{X}_t \)]
If \( \mathbf{X}_t \) has very small non-zero singular values (e.g., close to machine precision), Gram-Schmidt may treat them as zero due to finite precision, leading to an incorrect or collapsed basis. SVD identifies all non-zero singular values, ensuring \( \mathbf{U}_r \) spans the correct subspace.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Large \( n \) with Numerical Errors]
For large \( n \), Gram-Schmidt’s sequential orthonormalization of \( n \) columns amplifies floating-point errors, especially if \( \mathbf{X}_t \) is poorly scaled. SVD, implemented in robust libraries (e.g., LAPACK), maintains orthogonality, providing a stable \( \mathbf{U}_r \).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Pseudo-Class Partition]
\label{def:pseudo_partition}
Given a single-class dataset \( \mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \), we partition it into \( K \geq 2 \) clusters using the K-means algorithm. Each cluster \( C_i \) contains \( n_i \) samples with centroid \( \mathbf{m}_i \), and the global mean is computed as \( \mathbf{m} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=1}^n \mathbf{x}_k \). The centered data matrix is then defined as \( \mathbf{X}_t = [\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{m}, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n - \mathbf{m}] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \). For each sample \( \mathbf{x}_k \in C_i \), the within-cluster centered sample is given by \( \overline{\mathbf{x}}_k = \mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{m}_i \).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Scatter Matrices]\label{def:scatter_matrices}
Following Definition~\ref{def:pseudo_partition}, where we introduce pseudo-classes via K-means clustering, we redefine the key scatter matrices used in NFST.
\end{definition}

We first define the \textit{within-class scatter matrix} \( \mathbf{S}_w \), which captures the variance of samples relative to their corresponding cluster centroids:

\begin{align}
\mathbf{S}_w &= \sum_{k=1}^n \overline{\mathbf{x}}_k \overline{\mathbf{x}}_k^\top 
= \sum_{i=1}^K \sum_{\mathbf{x}_k \in C_i} (\mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{m}_i)(\mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{m}_i)^\top, \nonumber
\end{align}

Next, the \textit{between-class scatter matrix} \( \mathbf{S}_b \) measures the spread of cluster centroids around the global mean:

\begin{align}
\mathbf{S}_b &= \sum_{i=1}^K n_i (\mathbf{m}_i - \mathbf{m})(\mathbf{m}_i - \mathbf{m})^\top, \nonumber
\end{align}

Finally, the \textit{total scatter matrix} \( \mathbf{S}_t \) combines both sources of variance — intra- and inter-cluster — and can be computed as:

\begin{align}
\mathbf{S}_t &= \mathbf{X}_t \mathbf{X}_t^\top = \mathbf{S}_w + \mathbf{S}_b.
\end{align}


\textbf{Assumption 1} \textit{
We assume that \( \mathbf{X} \) is in general position: if \( n < d \), the samples are linearly independent; if \( n \geq d \), the data spans full variance across all subspaces.
}


\textbf{Corollary 1.} \textit{
Under Assumption 1 and a pseudo-class partition with \( K \) clusters, when \( n < d + K \), the rank of \( \mathbf{S}_w \) is sufficiently low to ensure at least \( K - 1 \) null projecting directions (NPDs).
}

\vspace{0.5em}
To address the lack of class labels in single-class learning, we introduce a clustering-based strategy to simulate a class-like structure. Specifically, we apply the K-means algorithm to partition the dataset into \( K \geq 2 \) compact clusters, referred to as pseudo-classes. This construction aligns with Definition~\ref{def:pseudo_partition} and enables the computation of the scatter matrices in Definition~\ref{def:scatter_matrices}. According to Corollary~1, the resulting cluster structure guarantees the existence of null projecting directions (NPDs) when the condition \( n < d + K \) is met, making the nullspace-based approach feasible. 

Algorithm~\ref{alg:kmeans-clustering} details the clustering process. After convergence, each cluster provides a local centroid \( \mathbf{m}_i \), while the entire dataset is centered using the global mean \( \mathbf{m} \). These components are then used to compute \( \mathbf{S}_w \), \( \mathbf{S}_b \), and \( \mathbf{S}_t \) as required for the XXX method.



\begin{algorithm}[!ht]
    \caption{K-means Pseudo-Class Partitioning for One-Class Learning}
    \label{alg:kmeans-clustering}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \REQUIRE Dataset \( \mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \), number of clusters \( K \)
        \ENSURE Clusters \( \{C_1, \ldots, C_K\} \), centroids \( \{\mathbf{m}_i\}_{i=1}^K \), global mean \( \mathbf{m} \)
        \STATE Initialize \( K \) centroids \( \{\mathbf{m}_i\}_{i=1}^K \) randomly
        \REPEAT
            \STATE Assign each sample \( \mathbf{x}_k \in \mathbf{X} \) to the nearest cluster \( C_i \) (Euclidean distance)
            \STATE Update centroids: \( \mathbf{m}_i = \frac{1}{n_i} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_k \in C_i} \mathbf{x}_k \) for all \( i \in \{1, \ldots, K\} \)
        \UNTIL{centroids converge}
        \STATE Compute global mean: \( \mathbf{m} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=1}^n \mathbf{x}_k \)
        \STATE Center the data: \( \mathbf{X}_t = [\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{m}, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n - \mathbf{m}] \)
        \RETURN \( \{C_1, \ldots, C_K\} \), \( \{\mathbf{m}_i\}_{i=1}^K \), \( \mathbf{m} \)
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}



\subsubsection{SVD-Based Null Projection in High Sample Regimes (\( n > d \))}

In high-sample settings where the number of training samples \( n \) exceeds the feature dimensionality \( d \), the original Null Foley-Sammon Transform (NFST) implementation suffers from numerical instability when relying on Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization. This instability degrades the quality of the null projecting directions (NPDs), as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:Compare}. 

To address this issue, we propose a robust enhancement — termed \textbf{ls-NFST} — that leverages Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to replace the Gram-Schmidt step. The use of SVD provides a numerically stable orthonormal basis of the total scatter space, while preserving the geometric structure of the data.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering  
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{pictures/svd_vs_gs_projection.png} 
    \caption{Comparison of SVD and Gram-Schmidt: SVD ensures stable convergence across different anomaly types, while Gram-Schmidt fails in high-sample regimes.}
    \label{fig:Compare}
\end{figure}

\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{definition}[SVD-Based Nullspace Construction]
\label{def:svd_improvement}
\textit{
Given the centered data matrix \( \mathbf{X}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \) with \( n > d \), we perform singular value decomposition as
\[
\mathbf{X}_t = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^\top,
\]
where \( \mathbf{U} = [\mathbf{u}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_d] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} \) contains orthonormal basis vectors for the column space of \( \mathbf{X}_t \), and \( \mathbf{U}_r \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r} \) (with \( r = \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}_t) = d \)) spans the total scatter subspace. This replaces the Gram-Schmidt step and is used to construct the projection subspace in ls-NFST.
}
\end{definition}


\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Lemma 1.}
\textit{
Let \( \mathbf{X}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \) be the centered data matrix with \( n > d \), and assume full rank along feature dimensions (i.e., Assumption 1 holds). Then \( \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}_t) = d \), and the columns of \( \mathbf{U}_r \) obtained from SVD form an orthonormal basis of the total scatter space.
}

\begin{proof}
Since the samples span all directions in \( \mathbb{R}^d \), and the matrix \( \mathbf{X}_t \) contains \( n > d \) centered vectors with general position, the row rank of \( \mathbf{X}_t \) is at most \( d \). If the data spans \( \mathbb{R}^d \), then \( \mathbf{X}_t \) has full row rank, hence \( \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{X}_t) = d \). The SVD decomposition produces orthonormal left singular vectors \( \mathbf{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} \), where the first \( d \) columns span the same column space as \( \mathbf{X}_t \). This gives a stable orthonormal basis without requiring iterative orthogonalization steps like Gram-Schmidt.
\end{proof}

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Corollary 2.}
\textit{
Given a pseudo-class partition into \( K \) clusters and a dataset with \( n > d \), there exist at least \( d + K - n \) null projecting directions (NPDs), provided the cluster structure is valid. This follows directly from the rank condition on \( \mathbf{S}_w \), as established in Theorem 2 of Ali and Chaudhuri (2023).
}

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Summary of Improvement.}
The replacement of Gram-Schmidt with SVD offers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Numerical stability in high-dimensional or large-sample settings;
    \item Guaranteed orthogonality and avoidance of floating-point accumulation errors;
    \item A consistent projection framework for both \( n < d \) and \( n > d \) regimes.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Algorithm~\ref{alg:ls-nfst}} summarizes the procedure for constructing the projection matrix in the large-sample case.

\begin{algorithm}[!ht]
    \caption{ls-NFST: Null Projection via SVD for Large-Sample Setting}
    \label{alg:ls-nfst}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \REQUIRE Centered data matrix \( \mathbf{X}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \), within-class scatter matrix \( \mathbf{S}_w \)
        \ENSURE Projection matrix \( \mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times L} \), projected data \( \mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times n} \)
        \STATE Perform SVD: \( \mathbf{X}_t = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^\top \)
        \STATE Extract the total scatter basis: \( \mathbf{U}_r \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} \)
        \STATE Compute: \( \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{U}_r^\top \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{U}_r \)
        \STATE Compute nullspace basis \( \mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times L} \) of \( \mathbf{A} \)
        \STATE Form projection matrix: \( \mathbf{W} = \mathbf{U}_r \mathbf{B} \)
        \STATE Project data: \( \mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{X} \)
        \RETURN \( \mathbf{W} \), \( \mathbf{Y} \)
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Anomaly Detection}

Once the projection matrix \( \mathbf{W} \) has been learned via XXX, the anomaly detection phase evaluates test samples based on their proximity to a set of reference points—termed \textit{singular points}—that characterize the normal data distribution in the projected space.

\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{definition}[Singular Points]
\label{def:singular_points}
\textit{
Given a set of cluster centroids \( \{\mathbf{m}_i\}_{i=1}^K \) from the training phase, the projected representation of each pseudo-class \( C_i \) is defined by a singular point \( \mathbf{y}_i = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{m}_i \) in the nullspace. These points serve as compact summaries of the normal class structure.
}
\end{definition}


\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{definition}[Anomaly Score]
\label{def:anomaly_score}
\textit{
For a new sample \( \mathbf{x}_{\text{test}} \in \mathbb{R}^d \), its projection into the nullspace is computed as \( \mathbf{y}_{\text{test}} = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x}_{\text{test}} \). The anomaly score is defined by the minimum Euclidean distance between \( \mathbf{y}_{\text{test}} \) and the set of singular points:
\[
\text{Score}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}}) = \min_{i=1,\ldots,K} \|\mathbf{y}_{\text{test}} - \mathbf{y}_i\|_2.
\]
A threshold \( \tau \) determines the classification: a test point is labeled as \textit{normal} if the score is below \( \tau \), and as \textit{anomalous} otherwise.
}
\end{definition}

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Threshold Selection.}
The threshold \( \tau \) is chosen to balance false positives and false negatives during anomaly detection. Empirically, as visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:threshold}, the optimal threshold depends on the number of pseudo-classes \( K \), with performance peaking when \( K \) sufficiently captures intra-class variation. We define \( \tau \) as the 100th percentile of the training scores:
\[
\tau = P_{100}(\{\text{Score}(\mathbf{x}_k) \mid \mathbf{x}_k \in \mathbf{X}\}),
\]
where \( P_{100} \) denotes the empirical maximum distance from training data to its nearest singular point. This choice avoids misclassification of training points while maintaining discriminative power against anomalies.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering  
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{pictures/threshold.png} 
    \caption{Threshold selection for anomaly detection: the optimal \( \tau \) aligns with peak AUC scores as \( K \) varies.}
    \label{fig:threshold}
\end{figure}

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Lemma 2.}
\textit{
The minimum inter-point distance between any two singular points,
\[
\delta = \min_{i \neq j} \|\mathbf{y}_i - \mathbf{y}_j\|_2,
\]
serves as an upper bound for the optimal threshold \( \tau \), ensuring that test points far from all pseudo-classes are correctly flagged as anomalies.
}

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Corollary 3.}
\textit{
If the affine hulls of the pseudo-classes intersect—i.e., the projected centroids are not well-separated—then fewer than \( K - 1 \) meaningful NPDs may exist. To preserve class separability and detection performance, it is necessary to re-tune \( K \), ensuring that the projected singular points remain distinct.
}


\subsection{Theoretical Analysis (Large Sample Size)}
\label{sec:method}

Prior work \cite{ali2022theoretical} gave \( d + c - n \) null directions for NFST, but we extend the analysis for IoT anomaly detection (\( n >> d \)) using clustering and three data properties (manifolds, grouping, periodicity) to derive XXX conditions via null space and cluster threshold.

\subsubsection{Proof of Existence when \( c \to n \)}

\label{}
We study the Null Foley-Sammon Transform (NFST) for IoT anomaly detection with \( n > d \), focusing on the null space intersection and cluster threshold \( c_{\text{th}} \) to ensure NFST exists as \( c \to n \).

\begin{definition}[Cluster Threshold]
\label{def:cluster_threshold}
\textit{
The cluster threshold \( c_{\text{th}} \) is defined as:
\[
c_{\text{th}} = n - d - k + 1,
\]
where:
\[
k = n - c - \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{P}_w),
\]
and \(\mathbf{P}_w = [\mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{m}_{j_k}]\) contains within-cluster centered data. NFST exists if \( c > c_{\text{th}} \), ensuring:
\[
\dim(\mathbf{N}_w \cap \mathbf{N}_t^\perp) \geq 1.
\]
}
\end{definition}

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Discussion on \( k \).} Given \(\text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_w) = \min(n - c, d)\), we evaluate \( k \):
\begin{itemize}
  \item For \( n - c > d \): \(\text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_w) = d\), so \( k = n - c - d \). As \( c \to n \), \( k \to 0 \), and \( c_{\text{th}} \to n - d + 1 \).
  \item For \( d > n - c \): \(\text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_w) = n - c\), so \( k = 0 \), and \( c_{\text{th}} = n - d + 1 \). As \( c \to n \), \(\text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_w) \to 0 \).
  \item As \( c \to n \): \(\text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_w) \to 0\), so \( k \to n - c \), and \( c_{\text{th}} \to c - d + 1 \), ensuring NFST exists.
\end{itemize}


\textbf{Lemma 2.} \textit{For \( n > d \), if data has non-zero variance in all directions:
\[
\text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_t) = d, \quad \dim(\mathbf{N}_w \cap \mathbf{N}_t^\perp) \geq d - \text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_w).
\]}

\begin{proof}
For \(\mathbf{P}_t = [\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{m}, \dots, \mathbf{x}_n - \mathbf{m}]\), non-zero variance gives \(\text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_t) = d\). The null space dimension is:
\[
\dim(\mathbf{N}_w \cap \mathbf{N}_t^\perp) \geq d - \text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_w),
\]
since \(\text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_t) = d\), \(\text{rank}(\mathbf{P}) \leq \text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_w) + d\).
\end{proof}



\textbf{Corollary 2.} \textit{If \(\text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_w) < d\), then \(\dim(\mathbf{N}_w \cap \mathbf{N}_t^\perp) \geq 1\). As \( c \to n \), \(\text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_w) \to 0\), ensuring NFST exists.}



\subsubsection{Discussion on IoT Data Distribution}
IoT data properties (low-dimensional manifolds, natural grouping, periodicity) lower \( c_{\text{th}} \), enabling NFST without requiring \( c \to n \).

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Assumption 1 (Low-Dimensional Manifolds).} \textit{IoT data lies on a manifold of dimension \( m \ll d \), constraining centered data to a low-dimensional subspace. Thus:
\[
\text{rank}(\mathbf{P}_w) \leq m,
\]
leading to:
\[
k \geq n - c - m, \quad c_{\text{th}} \leq c - d + m + 1.
\]}

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Assumption 2 (Natural Grouping).} \textit{IoT data forms compact clusters by natural grouping (e.g., devices), reducing variance. For cluster \( C_i \):
\[
\text{rank}(\mathbf{X}_i^C) \leq n_i - 1 - l_i,
\]
yielding:
\[
k \geq \sum_{i=1}^c l_i, \quad c_{\text{th}} \leq n - d - \sum_{i=1}^c l_i + 1.
\]}

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Assumption 3 (Periodicity).} \textit{Periodic IoT data with period \( T \) reduces cluster variance via correlation. For cluster \( C_i \):
\[
\text{rank}(\mathbf{X}_i^C) \leq n_i - 1 - l_i,
\]
resulting in:
\[
k \geq \sum_{i=1}^c l_i, \quad c_{\text{th}} \leq n - d - \sum_{i=1}^c l_i + 1.
\]}




\subsection{Implementation XXX}
In this section, we delve into the practical realization of the Localized One-Class Null Foley-Sammon Transform (XXX), outlining the training and testing processes that enable anomaly detection in single-class datasets. The implementation leverages clustering and SVD-based techniques to construct a robust projection framework, with detailed algorithms provided for both phases.

\subsubsection{Training process}

Based on the theoretical analysis provided earlier, Algorithm~\ref{alg:oc-nfst} outlines the step-by-step procedure for training XXX. The training process takes two inputs: the training dataset \( \mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \) and the predefined number of clusters \( K \). The training process includes two phases: partitioning the data into pseudo-classes using K-means clustering and training the discriminative algorithm to obtain the projection matrix \( \mathbf{W} \), the projected data \( \mathbf{Y} \), which represent the data in the nullspace. This phase establishes the foundation for anomaly detection by mapping the data into a discriminative nullspace.

The algorithm is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:oc-nfst}.

\begin{algorithm}
    \caption{One-Class NFST with Clustering and SVD}
    \label{alg:oc-nfst}
    \begin{algorithmic}
        \REQUIRE Data \( \mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \), number of clusters \( K \)
        \ENSURE Projection matrix \( \mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times L} \), projected data \( \mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times n} \)
        \STATE Apply K-means to partition \( \mathbf{X} \) into \( K \) clusters \( \{C_1, \ldots, C_K\} \)
        \STATE Compute cluster means \( \mathbf{m}_i \) and overall mean \( \mathbf{m} \)
        \STATE Compute \( \mathbf{S}_w = \sum_{i=1}^K \sum_{\mathbf{x}_k \in C_i} (\mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{m}_i)(\mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{m}_i)^\top \)
        \STATE Compute \( \mathbf{X}_t = [\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{m}, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n - \mathbf{m}] \)
        \STATE Perform SVD: \( \mathbf{X}_t = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^\top \), extract \( \mathbf{U}_r \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r} \)
        \STATE Compute \( \mathbf{B} \gets \) Nullspace basis of \( \mathbf{U}_r^\top \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{U}_r \)
        \STATE Compute \( \mathbf{W} = \mathbf{U}_r \mathbf{B} \), \( \mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{X} \)
        \RETURN \( \mathbf{W} \), \( \mathbf{Y} \)
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Testing process}

Based on the trained model, Algorithm~\ref{alg:test} outlines the step-by-step procedure for testing XXX. The testing process takes the new test sample \( \mathbf{x}_{\text{test}} \in \mathbb{R}^d \), the projection matrix \( \mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times L} \), the singular points \( \{\mathbf{y}_i = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{m}_i\}_{i=1}^K \), and the threshold \( \tau \). This testing process computes novelty scores for each sample, determined as follows:
\[
\text{Novelty Score}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}}) = \min_{i=1,\ldots,K} \|\mathbf{y}_{\text{test}} - \mathbf{y}_i\|_2,
\]
where \( \mathbf{y}_{\text{test}} \) is the projection of \( \mathbf{x}_{\text{test}} \) in the nullspace, calculated by \( \mathbf{y}_{\text{test}} = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x}_{\text{test}} \). After calculating the novelty scores, a hard threshold \( \tau \) is specified. In detail, we set the lower bound of the threshold to zero and the upper bound to the minimum distance between any two singular points in the nullspace. We then iterate through this range with a small step size (e.g., 0.01) to find the optimal \( \tau \). If the novelty score is higher than \( \tau \), \( \mathbf{x}_{\text{test}} \) is determined as an anomalous sample. Otherwise, it is classified as normal. This strategy aligns with previous research on NFST.

\textbf{Testing Algorithm (Algorithm~\ref{alg:test})}: The testing phase applies the trained model to new data.



\begin{algorithm}
    \caption{XXX Testing}
    \label{alg:test}
    \begin{algorithmic}
        \REQUIRE Projection matrix \( \mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times L} \), singular points \( \{\mathbf{y}_i = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{m}_i\}_{i=1}^K \), threshold \( \tau \), test sample \( \mathbf{x}_{\text{test}} \in \mathbb{R}^d \)
        \ENSURE Classification label (normal/anomalous)
        \STATE Project test sample: \( \mathbf{y}_{\text{test}} = \mathbf{W}^\top \mathbf{x}_{\text{test}} \)
        \STATE Compute anomaly score: \( \text{score} = \min_{i=1,\ldots,K} \|\mathbf{y}_{\text{test}} - \mathbf{y}_i\|_2 \)
        \STATE Classify: If \( \text{score} \leq \tau \), label as "normal"; else, label as "anomalous"
        \RETURN Label
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}







\subsection{Complexity Analysis}

The theoretical complexity of the proposed XXX algorithm can be divided into two main stages: clustering for data partitioning and computation of discriminant vectors using SVD-based NFST. The complexity of each stage is calculated using worst-case scenarios and is expressed in big-O notation.

\textbf{Clustering for Data Partitioning}: This stage involves applying K-means clustering to partition the dataset into \( K \) pseudo-classes. The process includes iterative assignment and mean updates:
- Initial assignment and distance computation for each of the \( n \) samples to \( K \) centroids: \( O(nKd) \).
- Iterative updates until convergence, with each iteration requiring \( O(nKd) \) operations, and typically converging in \( O(\log n) \) iterations: \( O(nKd \log n) \).
The combined complexity for this stage is thus \( O(nKd \log n) \), where \( d \) is the number of features and \( K \) is typically much smaller than \( n \).

\textbf{Computation of Discriminant Vectors}: This stage involves applying the SVD-based NFST to find the null projecting directions (NPDs) that map the data into the nullspace. The key steps include:
- Computing the centered data matrix \( \mathbf{X}_t \) and scatter matrix \( \mathbf{S}_w \): \( O(n d^2) \).
- Performing SVD on \( \mathbf{X}_t \) (where \( n > d \)): \( O(n d^2) \) as per standard SVD complexity for tall matrices.
- Computing the nullspace basis \( \mathbf{B} \) involves eigenvalue decomposition of \( \mathbf{U}_r^\top \mathbf{S}_w \mathbf{U}_r \), which is \( O(d^3) \) since \( r \leq d \).
- Matrix multiplications for \( \mathbf{W} \) and \( \mathbf{Y} \): \( O(d^2 L + d L n) \), where \( L \) is the number of NPDs and typically \( L \leq d \).
The overall complexity for this stage is dominated by \( O(n d^2) \) when \( n > d \).

\textbf{Sparse Matrix Considerations}: Although sparse matrix multiplication (SMM) could reduce complexity if \( \mathbf{X}_t \) or \( \mathbf{S}_w \) are sparse, we assume a dense matrix worst-case scenario, leading to \( O(n d^2) \) without significant reduction.

\textbf{Overall Complexity}: In conclusion, the theoretical complexity of the XXX algorithm is dominated by the \( O(n d^2 \log n) \) term from clustering when \( n \) is large relative to \( d \), resulting in an overall complexity of \( O(n d^2 \log n) \).

\section{Conclusion}
% ========================
This study introduces a novel algorithm called Local One-Class Null Foley-Sammon Transformation (XXX), designed to address the one-class novelty detection challenge within the realm of network intrusion detection for IoT environments. The XXX algorithm enhances the traditional Null Foley-Sammon Transformation (NFST) by applying K-means clustering to simulate pseudo-classes and leveraging Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) for stable subspace projection. This approach preserves both local and global data structures, mitigating the impact of singular points and improving accuracy when dealing with complex IoT network traffic. Moreover, XXX employs a robust projection framework to ensure accuracy and utilizes efficient matrix operations to reduce computational costs. Extensive experiments on various public datasets, including CICIoT2023, N-BaIoT, BoTIoT, and ToN-IoT, demonstrate the superiority of XXX compared to other baseline algorithms across diverse attack scenarios, achieving AUC-ROC scores of 99.61\% and AUC-PR scores of 99.47\%.

Besides representing a significant advancement in addressing the one-class novelty detection challenge, this research presents several open research directions. One key direction is the investigation of parallelization and hardware acceleration to enhance the computational efficiency of XXX, particularly in clustering and SVD computations. Another critical area for future work is detecting novel patterns in benign/normal traffic. Developing labeled datasets for benign traffic and refining methods to identify novel benign patterns will strengthen IDS performance in dynamic IoT network environments. 
Additionally, future research will focus on developing an adaptive mechanism for optimal threshold selection in novelty detection. By allowing thresholds to adjust dynamically based on dataset characteristics, it can further improve detection accuracy and minimize false positives, ensuring the model can effectively differentiate between novel and known traffic patterns.

\subsection{Additional Demonstration of Synthetic Anomalies}
\label{ap1:ano_types}

To evaluate the performance of our approach, we test it on 47 core benchmark datasets, which is similar to the datasets used in the original ADBench paper \cite{han2022adbench}. This allows us to assess how well the proposed methods work across a wide range of real-world scenarios and anomaly types. Below, we describe the generation process for four common types of anomalies.

\textbf{Definition and Generation Process of Four Common Types of Anomalies} \cite{han2022adbench}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Local Anomalies:} Local anomalies are points that deviate significantly from their local neighborhoods. To generate local anomalies, we first create synthetic normal samples using the GMM \cite{GMM}. Then, we scale the covariance matrix by a scaling parameter $\alpha = 5$ to generate local anomalies, which alters the covariance structure of the normal data.
    
    \item \textbf{Global Anomalies:}  Global anomalies are data points that are significantly different from the normal data. These anomalies are generated using a uniform distribution $\text{Unif}(min(X_k), max(X_k))$, where the boundaries are defined by the minimum and maximum values of an input feature $X_k$. The scaling parameter $\alpha = 1.1$ controls how extreme these anomalies are.
    
    \item \textbf{Dependency Anomalies:} Dependency anomalies refer to samples that do not follow the dependency structure of the normal data. In this case, the input features of the anomalies are assumed to be independent of each other. We apply the Vine Copula method  \cite{VinCP} to model the dependency structure of the original normal data. The probability density function (PDF) for generating anomalies is set to complete independence by removing the modeled dependency. Notably, because the Vine Copula \cite{VinCP} method can be computationally expensive when working with large datasets, we only use 2,000 normal samples to fit this model. After fitting the model, we use Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) \cite{KDE} to estimate the PDF of the features and generate independent anomaly samples.

    \item \textbf{Cluster Anomalies:} Cluster anomalies, also known as group anomalies, exhibit similar characteristics to clusters. To generate cluster anomalies, we adjust the mean feature vector of the normal samples by a scaling parameter $\alpha = 5$, i.e., shifting the mean of the GMM \cite{GMM}. This parameter controls the distance between anomaly clusters and the normal data. Then, the anomalies are generated by applying the scaled GMM \cite{GMM}.
\end{itemize}



Building on the approach from ADBench, we generate "realistic" synthetic data using a generative model like Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) \cite{GMM}, trained on the normal samples of a benchmark dataset. The original anomalies are discarded since their exact types are unknown. By adjusting the parameters of the generative model, we can create both normal samples and different types of anomalies. This method adds complexity to the generated anomalies, making the synthetic data better reflect the challenges of real-world anomaly detection tasks.

\subsection{Baseline Algorithms}
\label{ap1:ap_baselines}

In this section, we present the baseline algorithms employed for anomaly detection. These algorithms are categorized into two groups: \textit{Shallow Machine Learning-based} and \textit{Deep Learning-based} outlier detection algorithms. 

\subsubsection{Machine Learning-based Outlier Detection Algorithms}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clustering-Based Local Outlier Factor (CBLOF)} \cite{CBLOF}: CBLOF identifies anomalies by first partitioning the data into clusters and then evaluating the distance between the data points and the cluster centroids. Instances that exhibit significant distance from their respective clusters are considered outliers.

    \item \textbf{K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)} \cite{KNN}: KNN calculates anomaly scores based on the distance to the $k$-th nearest neighbor. Data points with larger distances from their nearest neighbors are assigned higher anomaly scores, suggesting that they are potential outliers.

    \item \textbf{Isolation Forest (IForest)} \cite{IForest}: Isolation Forest isolates observations by recursively partitioning the data using random features and split values. The anomalies, which are few and distinct, are isolated more efficiently, resulting in shorter path lengths.

    \item \textbf{One-Class Support Vector Machine (OCSVM)} \cite{OCSVM}: OCSVM constructs a decision boundary in a high-dimensional space, separating normal data points from outliers by maximizing the margin between the origin and the normal data points. The decision boundary thus defines the region where anomalies are unlikely to reside.

    \item \textbf{Local Outlier Factor (LOF)} \cite{LOF}: LOF measures the local density deviation of a data point in relation to its neighbors. Points exhibiting substantially lower density compared to their neighbors are considered anomalies.

    \item \textbf{Histogram-Based Outlier Detection (HBOS)} \cite{HBOS}: HBOS assumes the independence of the features and constructs histograms for each feature. Anomalies are identified based on the deviation of a data point from the expected distribution represented by these histograms.

    \item \textbf{Principal Component Analysis (PCA)} \cite{PCA}: PCA is employed to reduce the dimensionality of the data by projecting it onto a lower-dimensional space. Anomalies are detected by evaluating the reconstruction error, with larger errors indicating a poor fit to the reduced space and suggesting outlier behavior.

    \item \textbf{Subspace Outlier Detection (SOD)} \cite{SOD}: SOD detects outliers by examining various subspaces of the feature space. Anomalies are identified based on their deviation from expected behavior in one or more of these subspaces.

    \item \textbf{Connectivity-Based Outlier Factor (COF)} \cite{COF}: COF measures the chaining distance of a data point and its k-th nearest neighbors. Data points with unusually large or small chaining distances relative to their neighbors are classified as anomalies.

    \item \textbf{Lightweight Online Detector of Anomalies (LODA)} \cite{LODA}: LODA is an ensemble-based anomaly detection algorithm particularly suitable for real-time anomaly detection in streaming data. It is capable of adapting to concept drift and large-scale data environments.

    \item \textbf{Empirical Cumulative Distribution-Based Outlier Detection (ECOD)} \cite{ECOD}: ECOD uses the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) to model the distribution of each feature. Anomalies are detected by identifying data points that lie in the extreme tails of the distribution.

    \item \textbf{Copula-Based Outlier Detector (COPOD)} \cite{COPOD}: COPOD is a model-free, highly interpretable anomaly detection technique that uses copula-based models to capture the dependence structure between features. Detects anomalies in multivariate data without requiring hyperparameter tuning.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Deep Learning-based Outlier Detection Algorithms}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deep Support Vector Data Description (DeepSVDD)} \cite{DeepSVDD}: DeepSVDD uses a deep neural network to learn representations of the data while minimizing the volume of a hypersphere that encloses these representations. Data points that lie far from the center of this hypersphere are identified as anomalies.

    \item \textbf{AutoEncoder} \cite{Autoencoder}: AutoEncoders are neural networks designed to learn efficient representations of data through a process of encoding and decoding. Anomalies are detected based on reconstruction errors, with higher errors indicating instances that do not conform to the learned data distribution.

    \item \textbf{Deviation Networks (DevNet)} \cite{DevNet}: DevNet is a deep learning model that enforces a deviation-based loss function to penalize large deviations in the learned representations. This method is particularly effective in identifying outliers by ensuring that anomalous instances exhibit substantial deviations from the learned data distribution.

    \item \textbf{Learning Unsupervised Anomalies with Neural Representation learning (LUNAR)} \cite{LUNAR}: LUNAR is a deep learning-based, neural network approach designed for unsupervised anomaly detection. It learns a data distribution through representation learning and detects anomalies by identifying instances that deviate significantly from the normal patterns learned. This method is particularly effective in high-dimensional, complex data environments where labeled data is unavailable.



    \item \textbf{Autoencoder-based One-Class Support Vector Machine (AE1SVM)} \cite{AE1SVM}: AE1SVM is a hybrid method combining AutoEncoder-based feature learning with Support Vector Machines for enhanced anomaly detection. This approach leverages the reconstruction errors from the AutoEncoder in conjunction with SVM’s classification capabilities to detect outliers.

    \item \textbf{Adversarial Learned Anomaly Detection (ALAD)} \cite{ALAD}: ALAD (additional details to be included) is a deep learning model that utilizes adversarial learning techniques for anomaly detection. The architecture is designed to capture the subtle differences between normal and anomalous instances by learning the underlying data distribution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{ap1:EM}
To comprehensively assess the performance of the proposed method, we adopt a suite of evaluation metrics that capture various aspects of binary classification performance, especially relevant to anomaly detection in imbalanced and noisy IoT network traffic. The formulas and interpretations are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Accuracy (ACC):} Measures the proportion of correctly predicted instances among all samples.
    \[
    \text{ACC} = \frac{\text{TP} + \text{TN}}{\text{TP} + \text{TN} + \text{FP} + \text{FN}}
    \]

    \item \textbf{True Positive Rate (TPR) or Recall:} Indicates the ability to correctly identify actual anomalies.
    \[
    \text{TPR} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}}
    \]

    \item \textbf{False Positive Rate (FPR):} Represents the proportion of normal instances incorrectly classified as anomalies.
    \[
    \text{FPR} = \frac{\text{FP}}{\text{FP} + \text{TN}}
    \]

    \item \textbf{Positive Predictive Value (PPV) or Precision:} Measures how many of the instances predicted as anomalies are actually anomalous.
    \[
    \text{PPV} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}}
    \]

    \item \textbf{F1-score:} Harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a balanced metric for imbalanced datasets.
    \[
    \text{F1-score} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{TPR} \cdot \text{PPV}}{\text{TPR} + \text{PPV}}
    \]

    \item \textbf{Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC):} A correlation-based metric that considers all four values in the confusion matrix and is robust to imbalanced datasets.
    \[
    \text{MCC} = \frac{\text{TP} \cdot \text{TN} - \text{FP} \cdot \text{FN}}{\sqrt{(\text{TP} + \text{FP})(\text{TP} + \text{FN})(\text{TN} + \text{FP})(\text{TN} + \text{FN})}}
    \]

    \item \textbf{Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC-ROC):} Quantifies the model’s ability to distinguish between classes across various thresholds. It is computed as the area under the ROC curve, which plots TPR versus FPR.
    \[
    \text{AUC-ROC} = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \frac{\text{TPR}_{i+1} + \text{TPR}_i}{2} \cdot (\text{FPR}_{i+1} - \text{FPR}_i)
    \]
    A value closer to 1 indicates stronger class separation capability.

    \item \textbf{Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUC-PR):} Captures the trade-off between precision and recall, especially informative in scenarios with significant class imbalance. It is typically approximated using the trapezoidal rule:
    \[
    \text{AUC-PR} = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} (\text{Recall}_{i+1} - \text{Recall}_i) \cdot \frac{\text{Precision}_{i+1} + \text{Precision}_i}{2}
    \]
    Higher AUC-PR values reflect better performance in identifying positive (anomalous) instances.
\end{itemize}

This comprehensive set of metrics provides a robust foundation for evaluating the accuracy, reliability, and robustness of our proposed method under diverse and challenging IoT traffic scenarios.
